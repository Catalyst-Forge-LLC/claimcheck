# ClaimCheck — "STOP blaming your shower!!!"

**Source:** `post.md`, read 2026-11-11. Unchanged.
**Central claim:** > "So if you care about water that is actually used up, farms are the story, not your bathroom."
**Summary:** clear failures: 0 · partial: 1 · passed: 4 · N/A: 2
**Score:** 4.5 / 5 (2 N/A)

The one gap is recency: the post dates its figures to 2015 and they
match the report it cites, but USGS published newer estimates in 2025
and the post does not mention them. Mechanism, magnitude, boundary,
and evidence all hold: the post separates withdrawal from
consumption, sets each figure beside larger and smaller ones, states
that its totals are national, and names a primary report whose
figures were confirmed in this run. The newer estimates point the
same way as the central claim. This is a literacy review. It does not
rule the claim true or false.

## Claims read

- **C-1** (central) > "So if you care about water that is actually used up, farms are the story, not your bathroom."
- **C-2** > "public supply — the water utilities deliver to homes and businesses — was 39.0 billion gallons a day of withdrawals."
- **C-3** > "Irrigation was 118 billion."
- **C-4** > "Thermoelectric power withdrew 133 billion a day, but most of that goes right back to the river: only 4.31 billion a day was actually consumed."
- **C-5** > "Irrigation consumed 73.2 billion a day."
- **C-6** > "Those are national totals for 2015; your own watershed may look very different, so check your local utility."

## Findings

### 1 · Mechanistic literacy — passed

> "Thermoelectric power withdrew 133 billion a day, but most of that goes right back to the river: only 4.31 billion a day was actually consumed." · "water that is actually used up"

Claims: C-1, C-2, C-4, C-5
Why: Keeps withdrawal (taken and largely returned) apart from
consumption (not returned), and rests the conclusion on consumption.
It gives public supply as a withdrawal only and labels it as one;
consumption is a part of withdrawal, so setting irrigation's consumed
73.2 against public supply's withdrawn 39.0 understates the gap
rather than creating it.
Contrast: USGS Circular 1441 (2015 data), abstract: "Total
consumptive use for thermoelectric power was 4.31 Bgal/d in 2015 or 3
percent of the total thermoelectric-power withdrawals." Arithmetic:
4.31 ÷ 133 = 3.2%. The abstract gives consumptive use for
thermoelectric power and irrigation and none for public supply.
https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/cir1441
Next step: None.

### 2 · Quantitative and comparative literacy — passed

> "public supply … was 39.0 billion gallons a day of withdrawals. Irrigation was 118 billion. Thermoelectric power withdrew 133 billion a day … only 4.31 billion a day was actually consumed. Irrigation consumed 73.2 billion a day."

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-4, C-5
Why: Every figure sits beside a named larger and smaller case in the
same unit, and the comparison set includes the largest withdrawal
(thermoelectric) instead of leaving it out. The post gives no total
and no shares, but a reader can rank the three uses from what is on
the page.
Contrast: All five figures match the Circular 1441 abstract (2015
data): public supply 39.0, irrigation 118, thermoelectric 133 Bgal/d
withdrawn; 4.31 and 73.2 Bgal/d consumed. The same abstract puts
total withdrawals at "about 322 billion gallons per day." Shares, by
arithmetic: 39.0 ÷ 322 = 12.1%; 118 ÷ 322 = 36.6%; 133 ÷ 322 = 41.3%.
Ratios from the post's own numbers: 73.2 ÷ 39.0 = 1.9 (irrigation
consumed against public supply withdrawn); 73.2 ÷ 4.31 = 17.0
(irrigation consumed against thermoelectric consumed).
https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/cir1441
Next step: None.

### 3 · Scale and boundary literacy — passed

> "Those are national totals for 2015; your own watershed may look very different, so check your local utility."

Claims: C-1, C-6
Why: States the boundary of its numbers (national, one year) and
says the ranking may not hold in a given watershed. "Your bathroom"
is a slice of public supply, so comparing the whole of public supply
with irrigation does not narrow the boundary in the post's favor.
Contrast: USGS Professional Paper 1894-D (2025; water years 2010–20),
abstract, supports the caveat: withdrawals are "highly spatially
variable, with western States dominated by crop irrigation and
eastern States dominated by thermoelectric-power water use. Public
supply accounts for the largest percentage of water use in several
heavily populated northeastern States."
https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/pp1894D
Next step: None for the national claim. For a local one, see Next
steps, item 1.

### 6 · Recency and updating literacy — partial

> "In 2015, per the USGS national water-use report (Circular 1441)" · "Those are national totals for 2015"

Absent: any mention that USGS has published newer estimates for the
same three categories.

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-4, C-5
Why: The post dates its figures twice, which is the right half. The
other half is missing: Circular 1441 was posted in 2018, and a 2025
USGS report covers water years 2010–20. The newer figures do not
change the conclusion, so this is partial and not a clear failure.
Contrast: USGS Professional Paper 1894-D (posted January 15, 2025),
abstract, averages for water years 2010–20, conterminous U.S.:
public supply 35,400 Mgal/d withdrawn and 4,219 Mgal/d consumed; crop
irrigation 105,497 Mgal/d withdrawn and 75,698 Mgal/d consumed;
thermoelectric power from fresh water 82,656 Mgal/d withdrawn and
2,904 Mgal/d consumed. At 1,000 Mgal/d = 1 Bgal/d, irrigation
consumption is 75.7 Bgal/d (the post's 2015 figure: 73.2) and public
supply consumption is 4.2 Bgal/d. Arithmetic: 75,698 ÷ 4,219 = 17.9;
4,219 ÷ 35,400 = 11.9%. The two reports are not like for like: the
2025 figures are model estimates for the conterminous U.S., "crop
irrigation" only, and fresh water only for thermoelectric power.
https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/pp1894D
Next step: USGS Professional Paper 1894-D, tables 1–3 and 5–7 —
the water-year 2020 values for each category, and the report's note
on how its model estimates compare with the Circular series.

### 7 · Source and evidentiary literacy — passed

> "per the USGS national water-use report (Circular 1441)"

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-4, C-5
Why: Names the agency and the numbered report, a systematic national
compilation that a reader can open. Every figure in the post traces
to it.
Contrast: USGS Circular 1441, "Estimated use of water in the United
States in 2015" (Dieter and others, 2018), abstract. The five figures
in the post appear there as written.
https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/cir1441
