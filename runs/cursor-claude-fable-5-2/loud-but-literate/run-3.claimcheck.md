# ClaimCheck — "STOP blaming your shower!!!"

**Source:** `post.md` (undated post), read 2026-11-11. Unchanged.
**Central claim:** > "So if you care about water that is actually used up, farms are the story, not your bathroom."
**Summary:** clear failures: 0 · partial: 1 · passed: 4 · N/A: 2
**Score:** 4.5 / 5 (2 N/A)

No dimension carries a clear failure. The post separates withdrawal
from consumption, sets its figures beside larger ones, states that
they are national, and names a primary report whose numbers match.
The one gap is recency: every figure is for 2015, and USGS has since
published estimates for 2020, which point the same way. The concern
the post argues against, household water use in a stressed
watershed, survives, and the post's own last sentence leaves room
for it. This is a literacy review. It does not rule the claim true
or false.

## Claims read

- **C-1** (central) > "So if you care about water that is actually used up, farms are the story, not your bathroom."
- **C-2** > "In 2015, per the USGS national water-use report (Circular 1441), public supply — the water utilities deliver to homes and businesses — was 39.0 billion gallons a day of withdrawals."
- **C-3** > "Irrigation was 118 billion."
- **C-4** > "Thermoelectric power withdrew 133 billion a day, but most of that goes right back to the river: only 4.31 billion a day was actually consumed."
- **C-5** > "Irrigation consumed 73.2 billion a day."
- **C-6** > "Those are national totals for 2015; your own watershed may look very different, so check your local utility."

No side claims. The post proposes no remedy.

## Findings

### 1 · Mechanistic literacy — passed

> "most of that goes right back to the river: only 4.31 billion a day was actually consumed" · "water that is actually used up"

Claims: C-1, C-4, C-5
Why: Keeps use and loss apart. Each figure is labeled as withdrawn
or consumed, and the conclusion rests on the consumed ones.
Contrast: USGS Circular 1441 (2018, data for 2015) defines the same
split: water "consumed by livestock or humans, or otherwise removed
from the immediate water environment is termed consumptive use."
Its abstract gives thermoelectric consumptive use as "4.31 Bgal/d in
2015 or 3 percent of the total thermoelectric-power withdrawals"
(4.31 ÷ 133 ≈ 3.2%). It also says 72 percent of thermoelectric
surface-water withdrawals were freshwater, so part of the 133 is
saline water, not river water; the distinction the post draws is
unaffected. https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/cir1441 ·
https://pubs.usgs.gov/circ/1441/circ1441.pdf
Next step: None.

### 2 · Quantitative and comparative literacy — passed

> "public supply … was 39.0 billion gallons a day of withdrawals. Irrigation was 118 billion." · "Irrigation consumed 73.2 billion a day."

Claims: C-1, C-2, C-3, C-4, C-5
Why: Sets public supply beside two larger uses and gives both
withdrawal and consumption for those two. It gives no consumed
figure for public supply, but the comparison still closes:
irrigation's consumed 73.2 is more than public supply's whole
withdrawal of 39.0 (73.2 ÷ 39.0 ≈ 1.9), and "your bathroom" is only
part of public supply.
Contrast: All five figures match the Circular 1441 abstract (2015).
Against its total withdrawals of 322 Bgal/d: public supply 39.0 ÷
322 ≈ 12%, irrigation 118 ÷ 322 ≈ 37%, thermoelectric 133 ÷ 322 ≈
41%. The missing figure does not exist in that report: it
"reinstates the consumptive-use estimates for the
thermoelectric-power and irrigation categories" only. USGS's 2020
estimate for the lower 48 puts public-supply consumptive use at
"about 12% of the water withdrawn (4,241 mgd)."
https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/cir1441 ·
https://www.usgs.gov/mission-areas/water-resources/science/public-supply-water-use
Next step: For the shower itself, which the post never sizes: Water
Research Foundation, *Residential End Uses of Water, Version 2*
(2016), the indoor end-use shares table. Look for the shower's share
of household indoor use. Not opened in this run.

### 3 · Scale and boundary literacy — passed

> "Those are national totals for 2015; your own watershed may look very different, so check your local utility."

Claims: C-1, C-6
Why: States the boundary of its numbers and tells the reader the
national picture may not hold locally. National totals are used for
a national comparison and are not used to dismiss a local case.
Contrast: Circular 1441 (2015 data) supports the caveat. It is a
State and county compilation: "Data were not compiled for hydrologic
units (watersheds)." Use is uneven: "more than 50 percent of the
total withdrawals in the United States were accounted for by 12
States," and California alone "almost 9 percent … predominantly for
irrigation." https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/cir1441 ·
https://pubs.usgs.gov/circ/1441/circ1441.pdf
Next step: For any one place, the county rows of the USGS data
release "Estimated Use of Water in the United States County-Level
Data for 2015," or the 2020 subwatershed (HUC12) estimates, and the
local utility's water-supply report. Look for irrigation against
public supply in that county or watershed.

### 6 · Recency and updating literacy — partial

> "In 2015, per the USGS national water-use report (Circular 1441)" · "Those are national totals for 2015"

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-4, C-5, C-6
Why: Dates its figures, twice. It does not say that USGS has since
published estimates for 2020. Those give the same ordering, so the
conclusion stands either way: partial, not a clear failure.
Contrast: USGS estimates for 2020, lower 48 States only: public
supply withdrew "about 35,823 million gallons per day"; crop
irrigation withdrew "about 110,904 million gallons per day," of
which "approximately 72%" was consumptive use (0.72 × 110,904 ≈
79,900 mgd, or about 79.9 Bgal/d at 1,000 mgd = 1 Bgal/d);
thermoelectric power withdrew "about 59,889 million gallons per day
(mgd) of freshwater," with "about 3.9% … consumptive use (2,359
mgd)." Irrigation's consumed water is again larger than all
public-supply withdrawals (79,900 ÷ 35,823 ≈ 2.2). USGS cautions
that "2020 values are modeled estimates, so the change from 2015
partly reflects different methods." Circular 1441 was published
June 19, 2018.
https://www.usgs.gov/mission-areas/water-resources/science/public-supply-water-use ·
https://www.usgs.gov/mission-areas/water-resources/science/irrigation-water-use ·
https://www.usgs.gov/mission-areas/water-resources/science/thermoelectric-power-water-use
Next step: USGS Professional Paper 1894–D (Medalie and others,
2025), "Water use across the conterminous United States, water years
2010–20." Look for the 2020 withdrawal and consumptive-use totals by
category, and note that they cover the lower 48, not all States.

### 7 · Source and evidentiary literacy — passed

> "per the USGS national water-use report (Circular 1441)"

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-4, C-5
Why: Names the agency and the systematic report, which a reader can
open, and uses its figures within its stated scope.
Contrast: Each figure appears in the report's abstract for 2015:
"Public-supply withdrawals in 2015 were 39.0 Bgal/d"; "Irrigation
withdrawals were 118 Bgal/d in 2015"; "Withdrawals for
thermoelectric power were 133 Bgal/d in 2015"; "Total consumptive
use for thermoelectric power was 4.31 Bgal/d in 2015"; "Total
consumptive use for irrigation was 73.2 Bgal/d in 2015."
https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/cir1441

## A stronger version

To pass recency, the claim would give the USGS 2020 estimates beside
the 2015 ones and say that they cover the lower 48 and come from
models. It would also be firmer with a consumed figure for public
supply and a figure for showers, so that the comparison is consumed
against consumed and bathroom against farm, in place of a bound.
