# ClaimCheck — "STOP blaming your shower!!!"

**Source:** `post.md`, read 2026-11-11. Unchanged.
**Central claim:** > "So if you care about water that is actually used up, farms are the story, not your bathroom."
**Summary:** clear failures: 0 · partial: 1 · passed: 4 · N/A: 2
**Score:** 4.5 / 5 (2 N/A)

No dimension carries a clear failure. The post separates withdrawal from consumption, compares like units across larger and smaller uses, states that its totals are national and for 2015, and names a primary report whose abstract matches all five of its figures. The one gap is recency: in 2026 it rests on 2015 estimates without noting that USGS has published estimates through 2020 for the same three categories, though those point the same way. This is a literacy review and does not rule the claim true or false; the post's capitals and exclamation marks are a matter for a prose review, not this one.

## Findings

### 1 · Mechanistic literacy — passed

> "Thermoelectric power withdrew 133 billion a day, but most of that goes right back to the river: only 4.31 billion a day was actually consumed."

Claims: C-1, C-4, C-5
Why: Separates water that is withdrawn and returned from water that is consumed, and rests the central claim ("water that is actually used up") on the consumption figures. Public supply is given only as withdrawals and is labeled that way; read as a ceiling on what public supply could consume, 39.0 is still below irrigation's 73.2 consumed.
Contrast: USGS Circular 1441 abstract (2015 data, published 2018): "Withdrawals for thermoelectric power were 133 Bgal/d in 2015"; "Total consumptive use for thermoelectric power was 4.31 Bgal/d in 2015 or 3 percent of the total thermoelectric-power withdrawals"; "Total consumptive use for irrigation was 73.2 Bgal/d in 2015 or 62 percent of the total use (withdrawals and reclaimed wastewater)." Arithmetic: 4.31 ÷ 133 = 3.2%. https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/cir1441
Next step: None for this status. To replace the ceiling with a measured figure, see the public-supply consumptive-use estimate in USGS Professional Paper 1894-D (finding 6).

### 2 · Quantitative and comparative literacy — passed

> "was 39.0 billion gallons a day of withdrawals. Irrigation was 118 billion." · "only 4.31 billion a day was actually consumed. Irrigation consumed 73.2 billion a day."

Claims: C-1, C-2, C-3, C-4, C-5
Why: Every figure is in one unit for one year and sits beside a larger and a smaller case, including thermoelectric power, the largest withdrawal, with the reason it is not the largest consumer. The post gives no figure for showers or bathrooms and uses all of public supply in their place; that overstates the bathroom's side, so the conclusion does not depend on the missing number.
Contrast: Circular 1441 abstract confirms all five figures: public supply 39.0, irrigation 118, thermoelectric 133 Bgal/d withdrawn; 4.31 and 73.2 Bgal/d consumed (2015). It puts total withdrawals at "about 322 billion gallons per day." Shares of that total by arithmetic: 39.0 ÷ 322 = 12.1%; 118 ÷ 322 = 36.6%; 133 ÷ 322 = 41.3%. Ratios: 73.2 ÷ 39.0 = 1.9 (irrigation consumed vs public supply withdrawn); 73.2 ÷ 4.31 = 17.0 (irrigation consumed vs thermoelectric consumed). https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/cir1441
Next step: Optional. Circular 1441, Public Supply section, for domestic deliveries within the 39.0, to size household use inside public supply.

### 3 · Scale and boundary literacy — passed

> "Those are national totals for 2015; your own watershed may look very different, so check your local utility."

Claims: C-1, C-6
Why: States the boundary of its numbers (the nation, one year) and says they do not settle a local case. It also defines public supply as water delivered "to homes and businesses," so the reader can see the category is wider than households.
Contrast: USGS Professional Paper 1894-D abstract (2025): "Withdrawals for these categories of water use are highly spatially variable, with western States dominated by crop irrigation and eastern States dominated by thermoelectric-power water use. Public supply accounts for the largest percentage of water use in several heavily populated northeastern States." https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/pp1894D
Next step: The county-level data release for 2015 linked from the Circular 1441 page, and the reader's own utility's supply report, for the split between public supply and irrigation in one county or watershed.

### 6 · Recency and updating literacy — partial

> "In 2015, per the USGS national water-use report (Circular 1441)" · "Those are national totals for 2015"

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-4, C-5, C-6
Why: Dates its figures twice, so the reader knows their age. It does not note that USGS has since published estimates through 2020 for the same three categories. The newer figures point the same way, so the gap does not change the conclusion.
Contrast: USGS Professional Paper 1894-D (posted January 15, 2025), abstract, averages for water years 2010–20, conterminous United States: public supply 35,400 Mgal/d withdrawn and 4,219 Mgal/d consumed; crop irrigation 105,497 and 75,698 Mgal/d; thermoelectric power from fresh water 82,656 and 2,904 Mgal/d. Dividing by 1,000: 35.4 and 4.22; 105.5 and 75.7; 82.7 and 2.90 Bgal/d. Arithmetic: 75,698 ÷ 35,400 = 2.1; 75,698 ÷ 4,219 = 17.9; 4,219 ÷ 35,400 = 11.9%. Not like for like with Circular 1441: these are modeled, cover the lower 48 only, use water years, and count freshwater only for thermoelectric power. The Circular 1441 page names the report it supersedes (Open-File Report 2017–1131) and shows no later report superseding it. https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/pp1894D · https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/cir1441
Next step: Professional Paper 1894-D, the tables and text for water year 2020, for single-year withdrawals and consumptive use in each of the three categories.
