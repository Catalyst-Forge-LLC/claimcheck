# ClaimCheck — "STOP blaming your shower!!!"

**Source:** `post.md`, read 2026-11-11. Unchanged.
**Central claim:** > "So if you care about water that is actually used up, farms are the story, not your bathroom."
**Summary:** clear failures: 0 · partial: 1 · passed: 4 · N/A: 2
**Score:** 4.5 / 5 (2 N/A)

No dimension carries a clear failure. The one gap is recency: the post dates its figures to 2015, and the USGS has since published modeled estimates for 2020 that the post does not mention; the newer figures point the same way. The post separates withdrawal from consumption, sets its numbers beside each other, states its national boundary, and names a report whose figures match. This is a literacy review. It does not rule the claim true or false.

## Claims read

- **C-1** (central) > "So if you care about water that is actually used up, farms are the story, not your bathroom."
- **C-2** > "public supply — the water utilities deliver to homes and businesses — was 39.0 billion gallons a day of withdrawals."
- **C-3** > "Irrigation was 118 billion."
- **C-4** > "Thermoelectric power withdrew 133 billion a day, but most of that goes right back to the river: only 4.31 billion a day was actually consumed."
- **C-5** > "Irrigation consumed 73.2 billion a day."
- **C-6** > "Those are national totals for 2015; your own watershed may look very different, so check your local utility."

## Findings

### 1 · Mechanistic literacy — passed

> "Thermoelectric power withdrew 133 billion a day, but most of that goes right back to the river: only 4.31 billion a day was actually consumed." · "water that is actually used up"

Claims: C-1, C-4, C-5
Why: Keeps use and loss apart. It labels the 39.0, 118, and 133 figures as withdrawals, labels 4.31 and 73.2 as consumed, and rests its conclusion on the consumed figures. It gives no consumed figure for public supply, but consumption cannot exceed withdrawal, so the comparison in C-1 does not depend on it.
Contrast: USGS Circular 1441 (2018, data for 2015) abstract: "Total consumptive use for thermoelectric power was 4.31 Bgal/d in 2015 or 3 percent of the total thermoelectric-power withdrawals" and "Total consumptive use for irrigation was 73.2 Bgal/d in 2015 or 62 percent of the total use (withdrawals and reclaimed wastewater)." Arithmetic on the post's figures: 4.31 / 133 = 3.2%. https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/cir1441
Next step: None.

### 2 · Quantitative and comparative literacy — passed

> "public supply … was 39.0 billion gallons a day of withdrawals. Irrigation was 118 billion. Thermoelectric power withdrew 133 billion a day"

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-4, C-5
Why: Every figure sits beside larger and smaller cases of the same kind, in the same unit. The comparison set includes the largest category, thermoelectric power, and the post says why its withdrawal figure does not carry over to consumption.
Contrast: From the post's figures, 73.2 / 39.0 = 1.9, so irrigation's consumptive use in 2015 was about 1.9 times all public-supply withdrawals, and 73.2 / 4.31 = 17 times thermoelectric consumptive use. The post gives no national total; Circular 1441 puts 2015 withdrawals at "about 322 billion gallons per day" and says "Thermoelectric power, irrigation, and public-supply withdrawals accounted for 90 percent of total withdrawals in 2015" (39.0 + 118 + 133 = 290; 290 / 322 = 90%). https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/cir1441
Next step: None.

### 3 · Scale and boundary literacy — passed

> "Those are national totals for 2015; your own watershed may look very different, so check your local utility."

Claims: C-1, C-6
Why: National totals are used for a national claim, and the boundary is stated. The post tells the reader the national picture does not settle a local one.
Contrast: Circular 1441 (2015 data): "more than 50 percent of the total withdrawals in the United States were accounted for by 12 States," and California's 9 percent was "predominantly for irrigation." USGS Professional Paper 1894-D (2025, water years 2010–20): "western States dominated by crop irrigation and eastern States dominated by thermoelectric-power water use. Public supply accounts for the largest percentage of water use in several heavily populated northeastern States." https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/cir1441 · https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/pp1894D
Next step: For a local reading, the USGS 2020 estimates by subwatershed (HUC12) for public supply, crop irrigation, and thermoelectric power, and the local utility's own supply report.

### 6 · Recency and updating literacy — partial

> "In 2015, per the USGS national water-use report (Circular 1441)" · "Those are national totals for 2015"

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-4, C-5, C-6
Why: Dates every figure, twice, which is the right half. It does not note that the USGS has since published modeled estimates for 2020 for the same three categories. The newer figures point the same way, so the conclusion in C-1 is unchanged.
Contrast: USGS category pages, 2020 estimates for the lower 48 states: crop irrigation "about 110,904 million gallons per day (mgd)" withdrawn, "Approximately 72%" consumptive (110,904 × 0.72 ≈ 79,900 mgd, or about 79.9 billion gallons a day); public supply "about 35,823 million gallons per day," with "Consumptive use … about 12% of the water withdrawn (4,241 mgd)"; thermoelectric "about 59,889 million gallons per day (mgd) of freshwater," "About 3.9% … consumptive use (2,359 mgd)," and about 78,629 Mgal/d withdrawn with saline water included. USGS adds: "2020 values are modeled estimates, so the change from 2015 partly reflects different methods." The 2020 estimates cover the lower 48 states only; Circular 1441 remains the 2015 compilation. https://www.usgs.gov/mission-areas/water-resources/science/irrigation-water-use · https://www.usgs.gov/mission-areas/water-resources/science/public-supply-water-use · https://www.usgs.gov/mission-areas/water-resources/science/thermoelectric-power-water-use
Next step: USGS Professional Paper 1894-D (2025), "Estimated Water Use by Category," for the 2020 figures and how the modeled method differs from the 2015 compilation.

## A stronger version

To pass recency as well, the claim would need to say that the USGS has published modeled 2020 estimates for these three categories, give the 2020 withdrawal and consumptive-use figures beside the 2015 ones, and note that the two sets were produced by different methods. A consumed figure for public supply would let the central comparison be consumed against consumed.
