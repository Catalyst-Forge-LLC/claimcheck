# ClaimCheck — Swap the last incandescent bulbs

**Source:** `post.md`, read 2026-11-11. Unchanged.
**Central claim:** > "If you still have incandescent bulbs in the lamps you leave on for hours, swap them for LEDs."
**Summary:** clear failures: 0 · partial: 1 · passed: 6 · N/A: 0
**Score:** 6.5 / 7

The one gap is recency: the post's household baseline ("about 15%") comes from a DOE guide dated October 2010 that the post does not date, and EIA's latest end-use figure (2020) is about 6%. The other six dimensions pass: the post separates watts from lumens, scales the saving to the reader's own bulbs and hours, names the heating trade-off in both directions, and cites a guide a reader can open. The advice survives the stale baseline because the post already ties the saving to how many incandescents are left. This is a literacy review. It does not rule the claim true or false.

## Claims read

- **C-1** (central) > "If you still have incandescent bulbs in the lamps you leave on for hours, swap them for LEDs."
- **C-2** > "The U.S. Department of Energy says LEDs use at least 75 percent less energy than incandescent bulbs and last up to 25 times longer, for the same light."
- **C-3** > "Compare by lumens, not watts: an LED around 800 lumens replaces an old 60-watt bulb."
- **C-4** > "DOE's lighting guide says lighting is about 15% of an average home's electricity, so in a house still full of old bulbs it adds up."
- **C-5** > "How much it saves depends on how many incandescents you have left and how long they burn." · "In a home that already switched most fixtures, the remaining savings are small — start with the bulbs that are on three or more hours a day."
- **C-6** > "an incandescent's waste heat slightly warms the room, so in a cold climate some of the saving shows up as a little more heating. In summer it works the other way, and the air conditioner does less."

## Findings

### 1 · Mechanistic literacy — passed

> "Compare by lumens, not watts: an LED around 800 lumens replaces an old 60-watt bulb." · "an incandescent's waste heat slightly warms the room"

Claims: C-3, C-6
Why: Separates power drawn (watts) from light delivered (lumens), which is the distinction the swap rests on. It also treats the old bulb's heat as energy that stays in the room, not as energy that disappears.
Contrast: DOE, *Guide to Energy Efficient Lighting* (October 2010): "While a watt is a measure of electrical power, a lumen is a measure of light (brightness)." https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/guide_to_energy_efficient_lighting.pdf — ENERGY STAR, "Learn About Brightness" (page undated, opened 2026): a 60-watt old incandescent corresponds to 800 minimum lumens. https://www.energystar.gov/products/light_bulbs/learn-about-brightness — CMHC, Research Highlight 08-101 (January 2008): "between 89 to 96 per cent of lighting energy use is converted to heat and contributes to space heating as internal gains." https://publications.gc.ca/collections/collection_2008/cmhc-schl/nh18-22/NH18-22-108-101E.pdf
Next step: None.

### 2 · Quantitative and comparative literacy — passed

> "at least 75 percent less energy than incandescent bulbs … for the same light" · "How much it saves depends on how many incandescents you have left and how long they burn." · "the remaining savings are small"

Claims: C-2, C-5
Why: Gives a relative figure with its baseline named (an incandescent at the same light output) and scales it to the reader's own count of bulbs and hours of use. It says where the saving is small as well as where it is large. It gives no kWh or dollar figure, but it supplies the inputs to work one out.
Contrast: Derived from the post's own numbers (60-watt bulb, three hours a day, at least 75 percent less): 60 W × 3 h × 365 days = 65,700 Wh = 65.7 kWh a year for the old bulb; 65.7 × 0.75 = 49.3 kWh a year saved per bulb, at least. For scale against one measured case, CMHC (2008) modelled five 77 W bulbs at three hours a day replaced by 19 W lamps and reported 318 kWh a year saved: 5 × (77 − 19) W × 3 h × 365 = 317,550 Wh ≈ 318 kWh.
Next step: None needed for the classification. For dollars, multiply the kWh by the rate on the reader's own electricity bill.

### 3 · Scale and boundary literacy — passed

> "lighting is about 15% of an average home's electricity, so in a house still full of old bulbs it adds up. In a home that already switched most fixtures, the remaining savings are small" · "in a cold climate some of the saving shows up as a little more heating. In summer it works the other way"

Claims: C-4, C-5, C-6
Why: Keeps the average home apart from the reader's home and says the figure moves with how many old bulbs remain. It also separates the direct effect (lighting electricity) from the indirect one (heating and cooling), by climate and by season.
Contrast: EIA, 2024 RECS preliminary highlights (released September 17, 2026): 90% of U.S. households reported using LED bulbs, 37% used LEDs for all indoor lighting, and 5% used incandescent or halogen bulbs for all indoor lighting. Homes differ widely, which is the split the post draws. https://www.eia.gov/consumption/residential/ — CMHC (2008) Table 1 reports different net results by city, from Miami to Fairbanks.
Next step: None.

### 4 · Trade-off and substitution literacy — passed

> "One trade-off: an incandescent's waste heat slightly warms the room, so in a cold climate some of the saving shows up as a little more heating. In summer it works the other way, and the air conditioner does less."

Claims: C-1, C-6
Why: The post calls for a swap and says what happens instead, in both directions. It names the substitute (LEDs) and the heating side effect; it does not give the purchase price of the new bulbs. It gives the direction of the heating effect and not its size, and the conclusion stands either way in the source checked.
Contrast: CMHC, Research Highlight 08-101 (January 2008; compact fluorescents, not LEDs; R-2000 test houses plus a model of 33 North American locations). "For heating-dominated regions, the increase in annual space heating energy consumption (due to reduced internal heat gains) is about 0.6 to 1.7 per cent," which fits "a little more heating" as a share of the heating load. Against the lighting saving the offset is larger: "In a heating-dominated region, the 'take-back' effect associated with CFL reduced the cost benefits by up to 40 per cent, whereas in a cooling-dominated climate, there was an 'additive' effect, increasing the savings by up to 30 per cent." Table 1 lists overall benefit as a share of the lighting-only saving: Minneapolis 72%, Chicago 78%, Boston 86%, Los Angeles 116%, Miami 130%; some Canadian rows are lower than the text's 40 per cent reduction implies (St. John's with electric heat, 25%). "Overall, the cost savings are positive in all climates, although the interaction effects reduce savings in most cases." The swap modelled is a 75% cut in lighting power ((77 − 19) / 77 = 0.753), close to the post's "at least 75 percent." https://publications.gc.ca/collections/collection_2008/cmhc-schl/nh18-22/NH18-22-108-101E.pdf
Next step: The full Canadian Centre for Housing Technology report behind CMHC 08-101, Table 1: find the row for the nearest city and heating fuel. Then look for a repeat of the test with LEDs, and with a heat pump as the heating system, since both postdate 2008.

### 5 · Output and value accounting — passed

> "for the same light" · "an incandescent's waste heat slightly warms the room"

Claims: C-2, C-6
Why: Counts what the old bulb delivers. Light is held equal in the comparison, and the heat is credited as useful in cold weather instead of being written off as waste.
Contrast: DOE guide (October 2010): incandescent bulbs "waste 90% of their energy as heat." CMHC (2008): "On average, there is about 93 per cent utilization of heat from conventional lighting over the heating season." URLs as above.
Next step: None.

### 6 · Recency and updating literacy — partial

> "DOE's lighting guide says lighting is about 15% of an average home's electricity"

Claims: C-4
Why: The guide is dated October 2010. The post does not give that date and states the share in the present tense. EIA's most recent end-use figure, for 2020, is about 6%. The post already makes the saving depend on how many incandescents are left, so the stale baseline does not change the conclusion: partial, not clear failure.
Contrast: DOE guide: "Lighting accounts for about 15% of an average home's electricity use" and, in the footer, "DOE/EE-0344 • October 2010." https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/guide_to_energy_efficient_lighting.pdf — EIA FAQ (last updated March 15, 2024): "in 2020, electricity consumption for lighting accounted for about 6% (81 billion kilowatthours [kWh]) of electricity consumption in U.S. homes." https://www.eia.gov/tools/faqs/faq.php?id=99&t=3 — The 2020 figure is still the latest for end uses: EIA's 2024 RECS page lists the fuels and end-use tables as "Data will be released in spring 2027." https://www.eia.gov/consumption/residential/data/2024/ — The post's other DOE figures are not superseded downward: a DOE article dated May 5, 2023 says "LEDs consume 75% less energy and last up to 25 to 50 times longer than incandescent lightbulbs." https://www.energy.gov/articles/addressing-misinformation-doe-appliance-standards
Next step: EIA, Residential Energy Consumption Survey 2020, consumption and expenditures end-use tables, for lighting's share of household electricity. Recheck when the 2024 RECS end-use tables are released (EIA says spring 2027).

### 7 · Source and evidentiary literacy — passed

> "Source: DOE, *Guide to Energy Efficient Lighting* (DOE/EE-0344)."

Claims: C-2, C-4, C-5
Why: Names the agency, the title, and the report number, and the guide opens and carries the three figures attributed to it. Two things in the post are not in that guide: the 800-lumen equivalence (C-3) and the heating trade-off (C-6). Both check out against other sources, and neither carries a figure the conclusion depends on.
Contrast: DOE guide (October 2010): "LEDs use at least 75 percent less energy than incandescent bulbs, emit virtually no heat, and last 25 times longer"; "Lighting accounts for about 15% of an average home's electricity use"; "Replace bulbs that are lit three or more hours a day." The guide says "last 25 times longer" where the post says "up to 25 times longer." Its lumens example is a 75-watt bulb and 1,100 lumens, not 60 watts and 800. It does not discuss heating or cooling interaction. It is a consumer fact sheet and states the 15% share without naming the survey behind it. https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/guide_to_energy_efficient_lighting.pdf
Next step: For the household share, go past the fact sheet to the survey: EIA RECS, end-use consumption tables. For the trade-off paragraph, CMHC Research Highlight 08-101 and the full report behind it.

## A stronger version

To pass recency, the claim would date the guide (October 2010) and give the current household share next to it (EIA: about 6% in 2020), or drop the average-home share and lean on the per-bulb arithmetic it already sets up. It would be stronger still if the trade-off paragraph named a measured source and gave the size of the heating offset for a cold climate, and if the 800-lumen equivalence pointed to the table it comes from.
