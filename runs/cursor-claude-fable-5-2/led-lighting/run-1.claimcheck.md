# ClaimCheck — Swap the last incandescent bulbs

**Source:** `post.md`, read 2026-11-11. Unchanged.
**Central claim:** > "If you still have incandescent bulbs in the lamps you leave on for hours, swap them for LEDs."
**Summary:** clear failures: 0 · partial: 1 · passed: 6 · N/A: 0
**Score:** 6.5 / 7

Six dimensions pass and one is partial. The partial is recency: the
post's household baseline ("about 15%") comes from a 2010 DOE guide
that the post does not date, and EIA's 2020 survey puts the share at
about 6%. The advice does not depend on that figure, because the post
ties the saving to the reader's own bulbs and hours. This is a
literacy review. It does not rule the claim true or false.

## Claims read

- **C-1** (central) > "If you still have incandescent bulbs in the lamps you leave on for hours, swap them for LEDs."
- **C-2** > "The U.S. Department of Energy says LEDs use at least 75 percent less energy than incandescent bulbs and last up to 25 times longer, for the same light."
- **C-3** > "Compare by lumens, not watts: an LED around 800 lumens replaces an old 60-watt bulb."
- **C-4** > "DOE's lighting guide says lighting is about 15% of an average home's electricity, so in a house still full of old bulbs it adds up."
- **C-5** > "How much it saves depends on how many incandescents you have left and how long they burn."
- **C-6** > "In a home that already switched most fixtures, the remaining savings are small — start with the bulbs that are on three or more hours a day."
- **C-7** > "an incandescent's waste heat slightly warms the room, so in a cold climate some of the saving shows up as a little more heating. In summer it works the other way, and the air conditioner does less."

No side claims. C-2 to C-7 all support or qualify C-1.

## Findings

### 1 · Mechanistic literacy — passed

> "Compare by lumens, not watts" · "for the same light" · "an incandescent's waste heat slightly warms the room"

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-7
Why: Separates power drawn (watts) from light produced (lumens), and
states the saving at equal light output, which is the distinction the
advice rests on. It also treats the incandescent's non-light output
as heat that stays in the room, not as energy that vanishes.
Contrast: DOE, *Guide to Energy Efficient Lighting* (DOE/EE-0344,
October 2010): "While a watt is a measure of electrical power, a
lumen is a measure of light (brightness)," and "Incandescent bulbs
are based on old technology and waste 90% of their energy as heat."
https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/guide_to_energy_efficient_lighting.pdf
Next step: None.

### 2 · Quantitative and comparative literacy — passed

> "at least 75 percent less energy than incandescent bulbs" · "about 15% of an average home's electricity" · "How much it saves depends on how many incandescents you have left and how long they burn."

Claims: C-2, C-4, C-5, C-6
Why: The relative figure has a named baseline (an incandescent bulb
at the same light), the household figure is a share of a named total,
and the post scales both to the reader's own count of bulbs and hours
of use. It gives no absolute saving, but it supplies the inputs for
one; whether the 15% share is current is a recency question, taken up
in finding 6.
Contrast: Arithmetic from the post's own numbers (60-watt bulb, three
hours a day, 75 percent less). Old bulb: 60 W × 3 h × 365 days =
65,700 Wh = 65.7 kWh a year. LED at 75 percent less: 60 W × 0.25 =
15 W; 15 W × 3 h × 365 days = 16,425 Wh ≈ 16.4 kWh a year.
Difference: 65.7 − 16.4 ≈ 49.3 kWh a year per bulb. Because the post
says "at least" 75 percent and "three or more" hours, this is the low
end of what its numbers imply. The 60-watt and 800-lumen pairing is
confirmed by ENERGY STAR, "Learn About Brightness" (page undated,
opened 2026): 60 watts, 800 minimum lumens.
https://www.energystar.gov/products/light_bulbs/learn-about-brightness
Next step: None for the status. A reader who wants their own figure
needs the wattage printed on the old bulb, the watts on the LED's
Lighting Facts label, and their hours of use.

### 3 · Scale and boundary literacy — passed

> "about 15% of an average home's electricity" · "in a house still full of old bulbs it adds up. In a home that already switched most fixtures, the remaining savings are small" · "in a cold climate"

Claims: C-4, C-5, C-6, C-7
Why: Labels the 15% as an average-home figure and does not carry it
over to the reader's home; it says the result differs between a house
full of old bulbs and one that has mostly switched. It keeps the share
to electricity, not all household energy, and it places the indirect
heating and cooling effect inside the home and ties it to climate.
Contrast: EIA states its lighting share as a national figure for
"U.S. homes" (2020 survey data; FAQ last updated March 15, 2024).
https://www.eia.gov/tools/faqs/faq.php?id=99&t=3
Min (2014, dissertation abstract) on the heating and cooling side
effect: "The size of the effect depends on various regional factors
such as climate, electricity fuel mix, differences in emission
factors of main energy sources used for heating and cooling, and
electricity prices." https://doi.org/10.1184/r1/6716246
Next step: None.

### 4 · Trade-off and substitution literacy — passed

> "One trade-off: an incandescent's waste heat slightly warms the room, so in a cold climate some of the saving shows up as a little more heating. In summer it works the other way, and the air conditioner does less."

Claims: C-1, C-7
Why: The remedy names its substitute (an LED of equal lumens) and a
cost of the swap (more heating in a cold climate), along with the
reverse effect in summer. The size of the offset is given only as
"slightly" and "a little"; the post does not state the bulb's
purchase price. Neither changes the status: the swap is priced in
kind, and no position is taken here on the remedy.
Contrast: Min (2014, dissertation abstract), on single family
detached houses across the U.S.: "In some regions, these heating and
cooling effects from more efficient lighting can undermine up to 40%
of originally intended primary energy savings, erode anticipated
carbon savings completely, and lead to 30% less household monetary
savings than intended. However, this overall effect is at most one
percent of total emissions or energy consumption by a house."
https://doi.org/10.1184/r1/6716246 Only the abstract was read; see
**Unverified**.
Next step: Min (2014), Chapter 2, for the reader's own region: the
share of the lighting saving offset by extra heating, and how it
changes with the heating fuel.

### 5 · Output and value accounting — passed

> "for the same light" · "an LED around 800 lumens replaces an old 60-watt bulb" · "an incandescent's waste heat slightly warms the room"

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-7
Why: The thing the post objects to is the incandescent bulb, and it
counts what that bulb delivers: light, held equal across the swap by
comparing lumens, and heat, which it credits as useful in a cold
climate. It does not call the old bulb's whole energy use waste.
Contrast: DOE/EE-0344 (October 2010) says of the compact fluorescent
that it "produces the same amount of light as a comparable
incandescent"; its LED sentence gives the energy and life figures
without repeating the equal-light condition, and its "Lumens or
Watts?" section tells the buyer to match lumens.
https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/guide_to_energy_efficient_lighting.pdf
Next step: None.

### 6 · Recency and updating literacy — partial

> "DOE's lighting guide says lighting is about 15% of an average home's electricity" · "Source: DOE, *Guide to Energy Efficient Lighting* (DOE/EE-0344)."

Claims: C-4
Why: The post gives the guide's title and number but not its date,
which is October 2010, and a newer measurement exists: EIA's 2020
survey puts lighting at about 6% of home electricity. The baseline is
superseded, but the conclusion is unchanged, because the post itself
says the saving depends on how many old bulbs remain and is small in
a home that has mostly switched.
Contrast: DOE/EE-0344 is marked "DOE/EE-0344 • October 2010" and
says: "Lighting accounts for about 15% of an average home's
electricity use."
https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/guide_to_energy_efficient_lighting.pdf
EIA FAQ (last updated March 15, 2024): "According to the most recent
RECS, in 2020, electricity consumption for lighting accounted for
about 6% (81 billion kilowatthours [kWh]) of electricity consumption
in U.S. homes." https://www.eia.gov/tools/faqs/faq.php?id=99&t=3
Arithmetic: 6 ÷ 15 = 0.4, so the 2020 share is two-fifths of the 2010
guide's. The post's other DOE figures (C-2) are from the same 2010
guide and still sit inside DOE's newer wording: the July 2022 Energy
Saver guide (DOE/GO-102022-5740) says an ENERGY STAR certified LED
"uses up to 90% less energy than old incandescent bulbs" and that
they "last 15–25 times longer."
https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/2022-08/energy-saver-guide-2022.pdf
Next step: EIA, Residential Energy Consumption Survey 2020,
Consumption & Expenditures tables, "Detailed household site
electricity end-use consumption" (CE5.1a and CE5.1b for totals,
CE5.3a and CE5.3b for averages): the lighting row, for the share and
the kWh per household.

### 7 · Source and evidentiary literacy — passed

> "The U.S. Department of Energy says" · "DOE's lighting guide says" · "Source: DOE, *Guide to Energy Efficient Lighting* (DOE/EE-0344)."

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-4, C-6, C-7
Why: Names the agency and the document, with its number, so a reader
can open it, and attributes each figure to it in the text. The
guide's figures match the post's. Two statements are not in the cited
guide (the 800-lumen pairing in C-3 and the heating trade-off in
C-7); the first is printed on bulb packaging and confirmed below, the
second is stated without a number.
Contrast: DOE/EE-0344 (October 2010), opened in this run: "LEDs use
at least 75 percent less energy than incandescent bulbs, emit
virtually no heat, and last 25 times longer"; "Lighting accounts for
about 15% of an average home's electricity use"; "Replace bulbs that
are lit three or more hours a day." The guide says "25 times longer"
where the post says "up to 25 times longer." The guide's lumen
example is a 75-watt bulb and 1,100 lumens; the 60-watt and 800-lumen
pairing is in DOE's July 2022 guide ("To replace a 60 W equivalent,
look for a bulb with about 800 lumens") and ENERGY STAR's brightness
table. The 2010 guide is a consumer fact sheet: it states its figures
without methods or a citation for the 15%.
https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/guide_to_energy_efficient_lighting.pdf
https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/2022-08/energy-saver-guide-2022.pdf
Next step: None for the status. The measurement behind the household
share is EIA's Residential Energy Consumption Survey; see finding 6.

## Unverified

- The EIA survey tables themselves — only EIA's FAQ and the table index were opened, not tables CE5.1a to CE5.3b, so the lighting row and the per-household kWh were not read — EIA RECS 2020, Consumption & Expenditures tables.
- Where the 2010 guide's "about 15%" came from — the guide cites no survey or year for it — DOE/EE-0344's parent Energy Savers pages, or EIA's residential survey editions before 2010.
- The test basis for "at least 75 percent less energy" and "25 times longer" — the DOE guides state the figures without methods, and the specification was not opened — ENERGY STAR lamp specification; DOE's current Energy Saver LED lighting page, which returned "404 Not Found" at each energy.gov address tried in this run.
- The method and regional detail behind the heating-offset figures — only the abstract of Min (2014) was read, and it concerns "more efficient lighting" in general, not LEDs specifically — Chapter 2 of the dissertation, or the peer-reviewed paper drawn from it.
- The date of ENERGY STAR's brightness table — the page carries no date — ENERGY STAR, "Learn About Brightness."

None of these changes a status above.

## A stronger version

The claim would pass recency if it dated its baseline and set the
current one beside it: the 15% figure as DOE's from October 2010, and
EIA's 2020 survey figure of about 6% for U.S. homes. It already makes
the saving depend on the reader's own bulbs and hours, and that
condition would then rest on a current number. A reader in a cold
climate would also be better served by a figure for the heating
offset than by "a little."
