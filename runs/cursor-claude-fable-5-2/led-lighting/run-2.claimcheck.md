# ClaimCheck — Swap the last incandescent bulbs

**Source:** `post.md`, read 2026-11-11. Unchanged.
**Central claim:** > "If you still have incandescent bulbs in the lamps you leave on for hours, swap them for LEDs."
**Summary:** clear failures: 0 · partial: 1 · passed: 6 · N/A: 0
**Score:** 6.5 / 7

Recency carries the one problem: the post gives lighting's share of home
electricity from a 2010 guide in the present tense, and EIA's 2020 survey
puts that share at well under half the quoted figure. The other six
dimensions pass, because the post compares at equal light output, scales
the saving to the reader's own bulbs and hours, and names the heating and
cooling interaction. The post's own caveat about homes that have already
switched means its conclusion does not depend on the stale figure. This is
a literacy review. It does not rule the claim true or false.

## Claims read

- **C-1** (central) > "If you still have incandescent bulbs in the lamps you leave on for hours, swap them for LEDs."
- **C-2** > "The U.S. Department of Energy says LEDs use at least 75 percent less energy than incandescent bulbs and last up to 25 times longer, for the same light."
- **C-3** > "Compare by lumens, not watts: an LED around 800 lumens replaces an old 60-watt bulb."
- **C-4** > "DOE's lighting guide says lighting is about 15% of an average home's electricity, so in a house still full of old bulbs it adds up."
- **C-5** > "How much it saves depends on how many incandescents you have left and how long they burn."
- **C-6** > "In a home that already switched most fixtures, the remaining savings are small — start with the bulbs that are on three or more hours a day."
- **C-7** > "an incandescent's waste heat slightly warms the room, so in a cold climate some of the saving shows up as a little more heating. In summer it works the other way, and the air conditioner does less."

No side claims: each of C-2 to C-7 supports or qualifies C-1.

## Findings

### 1 · Mechanistic literacy — passed

> "Compare by lumens, not watts: an LED around 800 lumens replaces an old 60-watt bulb." · "for the same light"

Claims: C-2, C-3
Why: Separates power drawn (watts) from light produced (lumens) and makes
the energy comparison at equal light output, which is the distinction the
advice rests on. The trade-off paragraph also treats an incandescent's
heat as energy that ends up in the room, not energy that vanishes.
Contrast: DOE, *Guide to Energy Efficient Lighting* (October 2010): "While
a watt is a measure of electrical power, a lumen is a measure of light
(brightness)."
<https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/guide_to_energy_efficient_lighting.pdf>.
That guide's own pairing is a 75-watt bulb and 1,100 lumens. The
60-watt pairing is in an FTC brochure (undated; it says the labels start
in 2012): "A standard 60-watt incandescent bulb, for example, produces
about 800 lumens of light."
<https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-FT-PURL-gpo25713/pdf/GOVPUB-FT-PURL-gpo25713.pdf>
Next step: None.

### 2 · Quantitative and comparative literacy — passed

> "How much it saves depends on how many incandescents you have left and how long they burn." · "at least 75 percent less energy than incandescent bulbs"

Claims: C-2, C-5, C-6
Why: The relative figure comes with its baseline (an incandescent giving
the same light), and the post scales it to the reader's own count of bulbs
and hours instead of quoting a headline total. No absolute number is left
without a denominator. The share-of-home figure it uses as a second
baseline is out of date; that is taken up under dimension 6.
Contrast: The post's own inputs are enough to size one bulb. 60 W × 3
hours a day × 365 days = 65,700 Wh = 65.7 kWh a year; 75% of 65.7 kWh =
49.3 kWh a year saved, for a bulb at the three-hour threshold. Inputs: 60
watts and three hours from the post; 75% from the post and the DOE guide
(2010). No price per kWh was checked in this run.
Next step: The reader's own electricity bill for the rate per kWh, or EIA,
*Electric Power Monthly*, average residential price by state, to turn
kWh into dollars.

### 3 · Scale and boundary literacy — passed

> "lighting is about 15% of an average home's electricity, so in a house still full of old bulbs it adds up. In a home that already switched most fixtures, the remaining savings are small"

Claims: C-4, C-6, C-7
Why: Moves from a national average to the reader's own house and says the
average does not carry over to a home that has already switched. It also
steps outside the lamp to the heating and cooling system, and limits that
effect by climate and season.
Contrast: EIA, 2024 Residential Energy Consumption Survey, preliminary
housing characteristics (released 2026): "90% of U.S. households reported
using light-emitting diode (LED) bulbs," "Over one-third of households
(37%) used LED bulbs for all indoor lighting," and "5% of households
reported using incandescent or halogen bulbs" for all indoor lighting.
<https://www.eia.gov/consumption/residential/>. The post's "If you still
have" condition fits a country where most homes have already switched.
Next step: EIA, 2024 RECS housing characteristics, lighting tables (the
2020 edition numbers them HC5.1 to HC5.9), to see by climate region and
housing type where incandescents remain.

### 4 · Trade-off and substitution literacy — passed

> "One trade-off: an incandescent's waste heat slightly warms the room, so in a cold climate some of the saving shows up as a little more heating. In summer it works the other way, and the air conditioner does less."

Claims: C-1, C-7
Why: The central claim is a remedy, and the post says what happens instead
in both seasons. It gives no size for the effect and does not mention the
price of the replacement bulb; the rubric asks that the substitute or the
cost be named, and the substitute (an LED) and one cost (more heating)
are. No position is taken here on the swap.
Contrast: Parekh, "Benchmarking of Energy Savings Associated with Compact
Fluorescent Lighting in Homes," ACEEE Summer Study 2006 (Natural Resources
Canada; two matched test houses in Ottawa with gas furnaces, tests run in
2004, CFLs not LEDs): "During the heating season, the reductions in the
lighting energy use were almost offset by an increase in the space heating
requirements," and "The cost savings are positive in all climates although
the interaction effects reduce savings in most cases." Its modeled Table 4
(five 77 W lamps at three hours a day replaced by 19 W CFLs, 2005 energy
prices) puts net annual cost savings at 73% of the lighting-only figure in
Minneapolis and 80% in Chicago, and at 130% in Miami and 118% in Phoenix.
In those two cold cities the heating effect therefore takes 100 − 73 = 27%
and 100 − 80 = 20% of the lighting-only cost saving. The direction matches
the post in both seasons; "a little" is about a fifth to a quarter there.
<https://www.eceee.org/static/media/uploads/site-2/library/conference_proceedings/ACEEE_buildings/2006/Panel_9/p9_23/paper.pdf>
Next step: The same paper, Table 4, for the city nearest the reader. For
LEDs and for homes heated by electric resistance or a heat pump, open
Carnegie Mellon University, *Energy Efficient Lighting: Consumer
Preferences, Choices, and System Wide Effects*, chapter 2
(<https://doi.org/10.1184/r1/6716246>), and look for the share of the
lighting saving lost to heating by region.

### 5 · Output and value accounting — passed

> "last up to 25 times longer, for the same light" · "an LED around 800 lumens replaces an old 60-watt bulb"

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-7
Why: Counts what the old bulb delivers, which is light measured in lumens,
and holds it constant in the comparison. It also counts the by-product
heat as worth something in winter instead of calling all of it waste.
Contrast: FTC brochure: "If you're looking to buy a bulb that will give
you the amount of light you used to get from a 60-watt bulb, you'll now
look for 800 lumens."
<https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-FT-PURL-gpo25713/pdf/GOVPUB-FT-PURL-gpo25713.pdf>.
DOE guide (2010): incandescent bulbs "waste 90% of their energy as heat."
Next step: None.

### 6 · Recency and updating literacy — partial

> "DOE's lighting guide says lighting is about 15% of an average home's electricity"

Claims: C-4
Why: The guide is dated October 2010, and the post gives the figure in the
present tense with no year. EIA's 2020 survey puts lighting at about 6% of
home electricity, so the quoted share is 15 / 6 = 2.5 times the latest
measured one. The post's own caveat ("In a home that already switched most
fixtures, the remaining savings are small") keeps the advice standing, so
this is an error that does not change the conclusion.
Contrast: DOE guide: "Lighting accounts for about 15% of an average home's
electricity use," footer "DOE/EE-0344 • October 2010."
<https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/guide_to_energy_efficient_lighting.pdf>.
EIA FAQ (last updated March 15, 2024): "in 2020, electricity consumption
for lighting accounted for about 6% (81 billion kilowatthours [kWh]) of
electricity consumption in U.S. homes."
<https://www.eia.gov/tools/faqs/faq.php?id=99&t=3>. EIA's RECS page says
it "will be releasing the entire set of data from the 2024 RECS in spring
2027," so the 2020 figure is the latest end-use share available on
2026-11-11. <https://www.eia.gov/consumption/residential/>. The 75% and
25-times figures are also from the 2010 guide; a PNNL Building America
page viewed in 2026 still gives them: "ENERGY STAR certified LED products
use at least 75% less energy and last up to 25 times longer than
traditional incandescent lighting."
<https://basc.pnnl.gov/resource-guides/high-efficiency-lighting>
Next step: EIA, RECS 2020, consumption and expenditures tables,
electricity end-use tables (CE5 series), for lighting kWh against total
household electricity. Then the 2024 RECS consumption tables when EIA
releases them in spring 2027.

### 7 · Source and evidentiary literacy — passed

> "Source: DOE, *Guide to Energy Efficient Lighting* (DOE/EE-0344)."

Claims: C-2, C-4, C-6
Why: Names the agency, the title, and the document number, so a reader can
open it, and the 75%, 15%, and three-hour figures are in it. Two things in
the post are not in that guide, the 60-watt and 800-lumen pairing (C-3)
and the heating and cooling interaction (C-7), and neither carries a
figure the conclusion depends on.
Contrast: DOE guide (2010): "LEDs use at least 75 percent less energy than
incandescent bulbs, emit virtually no heat, and last 25 times longer"
(the post says "up to 25 times"; the guide has no "up to"); "Lighting
accounts for about 15% of an average home's electricity use"; "Replace
bulbs that are lit three or more hours a day."
<https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/guide_to_energy_efficient_lighting.pdf>.
The guide is a consumer fact sheet. It states these figures without a
method or a data source, so the measurement behind the 15% sits one step
further back.
Next step: EIA, Residential Energy Consumption Survey, for the measured
share of home electricity that goes to lighting. For C-7, the Parekh
(2006) paper named under dimension 4.

## Next steps

   It is the check most able to shrink the saving, and the post gives it no

   Mellon dissertation, chapter 2, for LEDs and other heating systems.
2. Replace the 15% share with the current one. Cheapest check: EIA FAQ,

3. Confirm the 2020 share at table level and watch for the next edition:
   EIA, RECS 2020 consumption and expenditures, electricity end-use tables

4. Check DOE's current consumer wording for "at least 75 percent" and "up

5. Read the Lighting Facts label on the replacement bulb for lumens,
   watts, and rated life, and compare with the 800-lumen and 25-times
   figures.

## A stronger version

The advice survives. A stronger version would date the 15% figure (2010)
and set EIA's 2020 figure of about 6% beside it, since the post already
says the saving is small in a home that has switched. It would give a
source for the 60-watt and 800-lumen pairing and for the heating and
cooling interaction, which the cited guide does not contain. And it would
put a size on "a little more heating" for a cold climate, by heating fuel.
