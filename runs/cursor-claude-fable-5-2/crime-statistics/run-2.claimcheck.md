# ClaimCheck — "Crime has never been this bad"

**Source:** `post.md`, read 2026-11-11. Unchanged.
**Central claim:** > "Crime is out of control. It is the worst it has ever been in this country"
**Summary:** clear failures: 2 · partial: 4 · passed: 0 · N/A: 1
**Score:** 2 / 6 (1 N/A, 1 unverified)

The problem sits mainly in magnitude and evidence: the post's one
national number has no rate and no earlier year beside it, and no
figure is traced to a measurement. Mechanism, boundary, and recency
are each half right. A legitimate concern survives: a rise in
murders in one city is checkable and may be real, and the post is
right that much crime is never reported to police. This is a
literacy review. It does not rule the claim true or false.

## Claims read

- **C-1** (central) > "Crime is out of control. It is the worst it has ever been in this country"
- **C-2** > "More than a million violent crimes were reported last year."
- **C-3** > "In my city, murders went from 30 to 40 in a single year. That is a 33 percent jump, and what is happening here is happening everywhere."
- **C-4** > "My neighborhood app shows a new break-in or car theft every single day. A few years ago I never saw any of that."
- **C-5** > "the official numbers can't be trusted anyway, because most crime never gets reported."
- **C-6** (side claim) > "The only answer is more police on every street, whatever it costs."

## Findings

### 1 · Mechanistic literacy — partial

> "the official numbers can't be trusted anyway, because most crime never gets reported." · "My neighborhood app shows a new break-in or car theft every single day. A few years ago I never saw any of that."

Claims: C-4, C-5
Why: Half right: police figures do count reported crime (C-2 says
"were reported"), and the unreported share is large. But a
separate official survey measures crime whether or not it was
reported, and under-reporting does not show which way crime is
moving. C-4 reads a feed of posts as a count of incidents without
saying whether the app's membership or coverage changed.
Contrast: BJS, *Criminal Victimization, 2024* (September 2025),
Table 4: 47.9% of violent victimizations and 30.5% of property
victimizations were reported to police in 2024. Unreported share:
100 − 47.9 = 52.1% (violent); 100 − 30.5 = 69.5% (property). The
same report's appendix table 1 gives the survey's total violent
victimization rate as 79.8 per 1,000 persons age 12 or older in
1993 and 23.3 in 2024; the reported-to-police rate went from 33.8
to 11.2 over the same years.
https://bjs.ojp.gov/document/cv24.pdf
Next step: BJS, *Criminal Victimization, 2024*, Table 4 (percent
reported to police) and Figure 1 with appendix table 1 (total and
reported rates, 1993–2024).

### 2 · Quantitative and comparative literacy — clear failure

> "More than a million violent crimes were reported last year. A million."

Claims: C-1, C-2
Why: An absolute number with no denominator and no earlier year
beside it. "The worst it has ever been" is a comparison across
years, and the post supplies one year. The 33 percent in C-3 does
state its base (30 to 40), but gives no population and no second
year of history.
Contrast: If "last year" is 2025, the count matches: the FBI
estimates 1,119,768 violent crime offenses in 2025, a rate of
327.6 per 100,000 inhabitants, down from 362.9 in 2024 (FBI, *UCR
Summary of Reported Crimes in the Nation, 2025*, August 2026).
Earlier years, from FBI, *Crime in the United States, 2010*,
Table 1: 469.0 per 100,000 in 2005; 758.2 in 1991, on 1,911,767
offenses. Arithmetic: 327.6 ÷ 758.2 = 0.43, so the 2025 rate is
about 43% of the 1991 rate; 1,119,768 ÷ 1,911,767 = 0.59, so the
2025 count is about 59% of the 1991 count. The older table labels
one category "Forcible rape" and the 2025 summary "Rape," so the
two series may not be strictly like for like. C-3's arithmetic is
right: (40 − 30) ÷ 30 = 33.3%.
https://cde.ucr.cjis.gov/LATEST/resources/reports/UCR_Summary_of_Reported_Crimes_in_the_Nation_2025.pdf
https://ucr.fbi.gov/crime-in-the-u.s/2010/crime-in-the-u.s.-2010/tables/10tbl01.xls
Next step: FBI Crime Data Explorer, *Crime in the United States,
2025*, Table 1 — the violent crime rate per 100,000 for every year
in the table, and the table notes on definition changes.

### 3 · Scale and boundary literacy — partial

> "In my city, murders went from 30 to 40 in a single year." · "what is happening here is happening everywhere."

Claims: C-1, C-3
Why: The first half states its boundary: one city, one year, and
it can be checked. The second half carries that city to the
country with no national figure, and a one-year change is offered
in support of "ever."
Contrast: Nationally, the FBI estimates murder and nonnegligent
manslaughter fell 18.1% from 2024 to 2025, to a rate of 4.1 per
100,000 (FBI, *UCR Summary of Reported Crimes in the Nation,
2025*). A national fall does not rule out a rise in one city; the
city's own figures were not checked in this run.
https://cde.ucr.cjis.gov/LATEST/resources/reports/UCR_Summary_of_Reported_Crimes_in_the_Nation_2025.pdf
Next step: the city police department's annual homicide counts
for at least ten years, with Census population for a rate; the
same city's row in the FBI Crime Data Explorer agency tables.

### 6 · Recency and updating literacy — partial · unverified

> "last year" · "in a single year" · "A few years ago"

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-4
Why: No figure carries a year and the post has no date, so a
reader cannot tell which release "last year" refers to or whether
a later one changed the picture. If "last year" is 2025, the
national count matches the latest FBI release, which keeps this at
partial.
Contrast: Latest editions as of 2026-11-11: FBI, *Reported Crimes
in the Nation, 2025*, released August 2026, which also revised the
2024 violent crime rate ("has been updated for inclusion in 'CIUS,
2025'"). BJS, *Preliminary Victimization Estimates from the NCVS,
2025*, published October 2026: 7.5 million violent victimizations,
25.7 per 1,000 persons age 12 or older, preliminary, from a
redesigned survey whose estimates "are not comparable to legacy
NCVS estimates." Final 2025 estimates are due in 2027.
https://bjs.ojp.gov/library/publications/preliminary-victimization-estimates-ncvs-2025
Next step: find the post's publication date, then match "last
year" to the FBI release for that year and check whether a later
release revised it.

### 7 · Source and evidentiary literacy — clear failure

> "More than a million violent crimes were reported last year." · "My neighborhood app shows a new break-in or car theft every single day." · "the official numbers can't be trusted anyway"

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-4, C-5
Why: No figure is traced to where it was measured: the national
count names no agency, the city figure names no city, and a
neighborhood feed with a personal recollection stands in for a
trend. The post sets official figures aside (C-5) while resting on
one (C-2), and does not name the survey that answers its
objection.
Contrast: The systematic reports exist and are public: FBI,
*Reported Crimes in the Nation, 2025*, drawing on 17,075 law
enforcement agencies covering 96.2% of the population; BJS,
*Criminal Victimization, 2024*, which "presents official estimates
of nonfatal criminal victimizations reported and not reported to
police" from a household survey. On C-4's subject, the FBI estimates burglary fell 15.8% and motor
vehicle theft 22.7% nationally from 2024 to 2025; that is a
national figure and says nothing about one neighborhood.
https://cde.ucr.cjis.gov/LATEST/resources/reports/UCR_Summary_of_Reported_Crimes_in_the_Nation_2025.pdf
https://bjs.ojp.gov/library/publications/criminal-victimization-2024
Next step: name the release behind the million figure; for C-4,
the local police department's incident data for burglary and motor
vehicle theft in that neighborhood, by year.

   Explorer, *Crime in the United States, 2025*, Table 1, violent

## A stronger version

A version that passed would stay with what the author can show.
It would name the city and the years, give murders as a rate per
100,000 across ten or more years, and either keep the claim local
or cite the national rate series by agency and table. It would
cite the victimization survey for the unreported share and say
what that survey shows over time, date every figure, and name
what the proposed policing would cost and what it would displace.
