# ClaimCheck — Crime has never been this bad

**Source:** `post.md`, read 2026-11-11. Unchanged.  
**Central claim:** > "It is the worst it has ever been in this country"  
**Summary:** clear failures: 3 · partial: 3 · passed: 0 · N/A: 1  
**Score:** 1.5 / 6 (1 N/A, 1 unverified)

The problem sits in magnitude, boundary, and evidence: the post offers a
raw count with no rate or earlier year, carries one city and one
neighborhood feed to the whole country and all of its history, and names
no report a reader can open. A legitimate concern survives. Under-reporting
is real, a rise in one city's murders matters to the people who live
there, and the national survey rate for 2024 was above its 2020 and 2021
level. This is a literacy review. It does not rule the claim true or false.

## Claims read

- **C-1** (central) > "Crime is out of control. It is the worst it has ever been in this country"
- **C-2** > "More than a million violent crimes were reported last year."
- **C-3** > "In my city, murders went from 30 to 40 in a single year. That is a 33 percent jump"
- **C-4** > "what is happening here is happening everywhere."
- **C-5** > "My neighborhood app shows a new break-in or car theft every single day. A few years ago I never saw any of that."
- **C-6** > "the official numbers can't be trusted anyway, because most crime never gets reported."
- **C-7** (side claim) > "The only answer is more police on every street, whatever it costs."

## Findings

### 1 · Mechanistic literacy — partial

> "More than a million violent crimes were reported last year." · "the official numbers can't be trusted anyway, because most crime never gets reported."

Claims: C-2, C-6  
Why: Half right. The post says "reported" for its count, and it is right
that a large share of crime is never reported. But it treats that gap as
making the numbers unusable, when a separate national survey counts crime
whether or not police heard of it, and a reporting gap alone does not show
which way crime is moving. It also leans on a reported count in one
paragraph and sets reported counts aside in the next.  
Contrast: BJS, *Criminal Victimization, 2024* (September 2025), p. 7 and
Table 4: "Approximately 48% of violent victimizations were reported to
police in 2024" (47.9%) and "30% of property victimizations were reported
to police." So about 52% of violent victimizations (100 − 47.9 = 52.1) and
about 70% of property victimizations (100 − 30 = 70) went unreported in
2024. The reported share was not lower in 2024 than in 1993: appendix
table 1 gives 33.8 reported out of 79.8 per 1,000 in 1993
(33.8 / 79.8 = 42%) and 11.2 out of 23.3 in 2024 (11.2 / 23.3 = 48%).
<https://bjs.ojp.gov/document/cv24.pdf>. The FBI's own summary (2025
data, p. 23) says the survey covers crimes "whether or not those crimes
were reported to law enforcement."  
Next step: BJS, *Criminal Victimization, 2024*, Table 4 (percent reported
to police, by type of crime) and appendix table 1 (total and reported
rates, 1993–2024). Look for whether the reported share fell in the years
the post is describing.

### 2 · Quantitative and comparative literacy — clear failure

> "More than a million violent crimes were reported last year. A million."

Claims: C-1, C-2  
Why: An absolute count with no rate, no population, and no earlier year,
offered in support of "the worst it has ever been." That conclusion is a
comparison over time, and the post gives the reader nothing to compare
with. The 33 percent figure in C-3 does show its baseline (30 to 40;
10 / 30 = 33.3%), so the fault is in the national number.  
Contrast: FBI, *UCR Summary of Reported Crimes in the Nation, 2025*
(released August 2026), p. 7: "An estimated 1,119,768 violent crime
offenses were committed in 2025, indicating an estimated offense rate of
327.6 violent crimes per 100,000 inhabitants, down from the 2024
estimated offense rate of 362.9 violent crimes per 100,000 inhabitants."
The count matches the post if "last year" means 2025. Check against the enrolled population in Table 1, p. 6:
1,119,768 / 341,784,857 × 100,000 = 327.6. Three comparisons:
(a) 2024: 362.9 per 100,000, so 2025 is (362.9 − 327.6) / 362.9 = 9.7%
lower. (b) 2006: the four rates in Figure 1 sum to
5.7 + 29.8 + 150.1 + 291.7 = 477.3 per 100,000, and 327.6 / 477.3 = 0.69,
about 31% lower; the 2006 rape rate uses the narrower definition in
force before 2013 (footnote 6). (c) The BJS survey, which includes
unreported crime: 79.8 violent victimizations per 1,000 persons age 12
or older in 1993 and 23.3 in 2024; 23.3 / 79.8 = 0.29, about 71% lower.
<https://cde.ucr.cjis.gov/LATEST/resources/reports/UCR_Summary_of_Reported_Crimes_in_the_Nation_2025.pdf>,
<https://bjs.ojp.gov/document/cv24.pdf>  
Next step: FBI Crime Data Explorer, *Crime in the United States, 2025*,
Table 1 (estimated volume and rate by year). The summary opened here
charts 2006–2025 only. Look for the violent crime rate in each earlier
year and for the highest year on record.

### 3 · Scale and boundary literacy — clear failure

> "In my city, murders went from 30 to 40 in a single year." · "what is happening here is happening everywhere."

Claims: C-1, C-3, C-4, C-5  
Why: One city's one-year change and one neighborhood's feed are carried
to the whole country and to all of its history. The central claim is
national and all-time, the evidence is local and spans one to a few
years, and the conclusion rests on that step.  
Contrast: FBI, *UCR Summary of Reported Crimes in the Nation, 2025*,
p. 7: nationwide from 2024 to 2025, "Murder and nonnegligent
manslaughter decreased an estimated 18.1%." P. 13: "No population group
designated within 'CIUS, 2025' showed an increase in the year-to-year
violent crime trend." A group average does not rule out a rise in a
single city, so the local figure may be right. It cannot stand in for
the country. The city is not named, so its counts were not checked in
this run.  
Next step: the named city's police department homicide counts for the
last ten years, with population, and the same agency's entry in the FBI
Crime Data Explorer. Look for murders per 100,000 by year, and whether
40 is high against the city's own record.

### 6 · Recency and updating literacy — partial · unverified

> "last year" · "in a single year" · "A few years ago"

Claims: C-1, C-2, C-3, C-5  
Why: No figure carries a year and the post has no date, so a reader
cannot tell which edition a number comes from or whether a newer one
exists. The million figure is consistent with the newest FBI estimate,
which is the half that holds. The present-tense "worst it has ever been"
is tied to no dated measurement.  
Contrast: The newest editions as of this run (November 2026) are the
FBI's 2025 data, released August 2026, and BJS's preliminary 2025 survey
estimates, released October 2026. The FBI summary, p. 7, puts the 2025
murder rate at 4.1 per 100,000, "tied for lowest rates recorded since
national estimates began" in 1936, and its preview on p. 4 reports
violent crime "down 10.6%" for January–June 2026 against the same months
of 2025 (preliminary). BJS reports 25.7 violent victimizations per 1,000
in 2025 against 28.7 in 2024 on its redesigned survey, and states that
those estimates "are not comparable to legacy NCVS estimates" such as
the 23.3 for 2024 quoted above.
<https://bjs.ojp.gov/document/pvencvs25.pdf>. Which year the post means:
not checked in this run.  
Next step: establish when the post was written. Then open the FBI annual
release for the year it calls "last year" and the release after it, and
look for whether the direction changed.

### 7 · Source and evidentiary literacy — clear failure

> "My neighborhood app shows a new break-in or car theft every single day. A few years ago I never saw any of that." · "the official numbers can't be trusted anyway"

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-5, C-6  
Why: No figure is traced to anything a reader can open. The million has
no agency or report behind it and the city has no name. The one source
the post does identify is an app feed, which shows the posts the author
saw, and the post prefers it to the systematic reports it sets aside.  
Contrast: Systematic reports on both measures exist. Reported crime:
FBI summary, p. 8, has burglary down an estimated 15.8% and motor
vehicle theft down an estimated 22.7% nationwide from 2024 to 2025.
Survey, including unreported crime: BJS preliminary 2025 estimates have
property victimization at 138.0 per 1,000 households in 2024 and 111.2
in 2025. Neither describes the author's neighborhood. The app's post
counts were not checked in this run.  
Next step: the local police department's published incident data for the
neighborhood's reporting district. Look for burglaries and motor vehicle
thefts by year, and compare with how many people in the neighborhood
were posting on the app in the same years.

## A stronger version

A version that kept the concern would name the city and the years, give
murders per 100,000 for several years and not one pair of counts, and
say whether the local direction matches the national one. It would cite
the survey that counts unreported crime where it now dismisses the
official numbers; that survey's 2024 rate (23.3 per 1,000) was above
its 2020 rate (16.4), a rise of 23.3 / 16.4 = 1.42, about 42%, which is
a checkable claim about recent years. It would use police incident data
for the neighborhood in place of the app, limit the claim to the place
and period the data cover, and put a price on the remedy.
