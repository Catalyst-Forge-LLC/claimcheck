# ClaimCheck — "Crime has never been this bad"

**Source:** `post.md`, read 2026-11-11. Unchanged.  
**Central claim:** > "Crime is out of control. It is the worst it has ever been in this country"  
**Summary:** clear failures: 3 · partial: 3 · passed: 0 · N/A: 1  
**Score:** 1.5 / 6 (1 N/A)

The problem sits in magnitude, boundary, and evidence: a national count with no rate or earlier year beside it, one city's single-year change carried to "everywhere," and a neighborhood feed preferred over the systematic reports. Mechanism, recency, and trade-off are half handled. A legitimate concern survives: a rise in murders in one city is checkable and can be real whatever the national series shows, and the post is right that police figures miss crime nobody reports. This is a literacy review. It does not rule the claim true or false.

## Claims read

- **C-1** (central) > "Crime is out of control. It is the worst it has ever been in this country"
- **C-2** > "More than a million violent crimes were reported last year. A million."
- **C-3** > "In my city, murders went from 30 to 40 in a single year. That is a 33 percent jump"
- **C-4** > "what is happening here is happening everywhere."
- **C-5** > "My neighborhood app shows a new break-in or car theft every single day. A few years ago I never saw any of that."
- **C-6** > "the official numbers can't be trusted anyway, because most crime never gets reported."
- **C-7** (side claim) > "The only answer is more police on every street, whatever it costs."

## Findings

### 1 · Mechanistic literacy — partial

> "More than a million violent crimes were reported last year." · "the official numbers can't be trusted anyway, because most crime never gets reported." · "My neighborhood app shows a new break-in or car theft every single day. A few years ago I never saw any of that."

Claims: C-2, C-5, C-6
Why: Half right. C-2 says "reported," which is what police figures count, and C-6 is right that they miss unreported crime. But a separate survey measures exactly that gap, under-reporting does not show which way crime is moving, and C-5 reads posts seen in an app as crimes that occurred.
Contrast: The FBI's figures are offenses "reported to the FBI by participating law enforcement agencies" (FBI press release, 14 Aug 2026, https://www.fbi.gov/news/press-releases/fbi-releases-2025-reported-crimes-in-the-nation-statistics). The BJS victimization survey covers crimes "reported and not reported to police": in 2024 it found 23.3 violent victimizations per 1,000 persons age 12 or older, of which 11.2 per 1,000 were reported to police (BJS, *Criminal Victimization, 2024*, https://bjs.ojp.gov/library/publications/criminal-victimization-2024). 11.2 ÷ 23.3 = 48.1% reported, so about 52% of violent victimizations were not. The share for property crime was not checked in this run.
Next step: BJS, *Criminal Victimization, 2024*, full report, the tables on victimizations reported to police, for the violent and property shares by crime type. Then the survey's own year-to-year trend, which includes unreported crime.

### 2 · Quantitative and comparative literacy — clear failure

> "It is the worst it has ever been in this country" · "More than a million violent crimes were reported last year. A million."

Claims: C-1, C-2, C-3
Why: An absolute count with no rate and no earlier year beside it, used to support "worst ever," which is a comparison across all years. C-3 does give its base (30 to 40 is a 33 percent rise: 10 ÷ 30 = 33.3%) but gives no population, so no rate, and no year before the 30.
Contrast: The FBI estimates 1,119,768 violent crimes in 2025, a rate of 327.6 per 100,000 inhabitants, down from 362.9 in 2024 (FBI, *UCR Summary of Reported Crimes in the Nation, 2025*, https://cde.ucr.cjis.gov/LATEST/resources/reports/UCR_Summary_of_Reported_Crimes_in_the_Nation_2025.pdf). The denominator that count implies is 1,119,768 ÷ 327.6 × 100,000 ≈ 341.8 million people. Three comparisons:
- Violent crime rate, 1991: 1,911,767 offenses, 758.2 per 100,000 (FBI, *Crime in the United States, 2010*, Table 1, https://ucr.fbi.gov/crime-in-the-u.s/2010/crime-in-the-u.s.-2010/tables/10tbl01.xls). 327.6 ÷ 758.2 = 0.43, so the 2025 rate is about 57% below the 1991 rate.
- Murder rate: 9.8 per 100,000 in 1991 (same table) and 4.1 in 2025 (FBI summary, 2025). 4.1 ÷ 9.8 = 0.42.
- Count alone: every year from 1991 to 2019 in the two FBI tables opened shows more than a million violent crimes; the lowest is 1,153,022 in 2014 (FBI, *Crime in the United States, 2019*, Table 1, https://ucr.fbi.gov/crime-in-the-u.s/2019/crime-in-the-u.s.-2019/topic-pages/tables/table-1). "More than a million" does not separate a high year from a low one.

The 2019 table notes that its violent crime totals use the legacy definition of rape. Which definition the 2025 total uses was not checked, so read the long-run ratios as approximate.
Next step: FBI Crime Data Explorer, national violent crime rate per 100,000 by year, for the full series including 2020–2024 and the years before 1991. For C-3, the city's population and its murder counts for the previous ten years.

### 3 · Scale and boundary literacy — clear failure

> "In my city, murders went from 30 to 40 in a single year. That is a 33 percent jump, and what is happening here is happening everywhere."

Claims: C-1, C-3, C-4, C-5
Why: One city, one offense, one year is carried to the whole country and to "ever," and one neighborhood's feed is added to it. The central claim is national and all-time; its local evidence is neither. This is a local problem used to describe a national total.
Contrast: Nationally, estimated murder and nonnegligent manslaughter fell 18.1% from 2024 to 2025, burglary fell 15.8%, and motor vehicle theft fell 22.7% (FBI, *UCR Summary of Reported Crimes in the Nation, 2025*, URL above). A national fall does not rule out a rise in one city. The city's figures were not checked in this run: the post does not name the city or the year.
Next step: the city police department's annual homicide counts, or the agency-level table for that city in the FBI Crime Data Explorer, set beside the national series for the same years.

### 4 · Trade-off and substitution literacy — partial

Contrast: not checked in this run.
Next step: the city's adopted budget, police department section: current sworn headcount, cost per added officer, and what the general fund would give up.

### 6 · Recency and updating literacy — partial

> "More than a million violent crimes were reported last year." · "It is the worst it has ever been" · "A few years ago I never saw any of that."

Claims: C-1, C-2, C-5
Why: The million figure is in line with the latest release, but no figure in the post carries a year and the post itself is undated, so a reader cannot tell which year "last year" or "a few years ago" is. The present-tense "is the worst" is not set against the newest data, which had moved the other way by the date of this review.
Contrast: The latest national release covers 2025 and came out in August 2026. It reports "the largest year-to-year decline in violent crime rates since FBI estimations began in 1936" and a 2025 murder rate of 4.1 per 100,000, "tied with 1955 and 1956 for the lowest murder rate" (FBI press release, 14 Aug 2026, URL above). The same summary puts the 20-year high for the murder rate at 6.6 per 100,000, in 2020 and 2022 (FBI, *UCR Summary of Reported Crimes in the Nation, 2025*, URL above). Preliminary survey estimates for 2025, published October 2026, show violent crime excluding simple assault falling from 9.9 to 7.4 per 1,000 persons age 12 or older from 2024 to 2025 (U.S. Department of Justice press release on the BJS preliminary estimates, 26 Oct 2026, https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/national-crime-victimization-survey-shows-violent-crime-plummeted-2025).
Next step: date the post and each figure, then open the FBI release for that year and the one after it. If the post dates from 2020–2022, compare it with the releases for those years, when the murder rate was at its 20-year high.

### 7 · Source and evidentiary literacy — clear failure

> "My neighborhood app shows a new break-in or car theft every single day. A few years ago I never saw any of that. And the official numbers can't be trusted anyway"

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-5, C-6
Why: No figure is traced to where it was measured: the million has no agency or report, the city's murder counts have no source, and the property-crime evidence is a feed plus recollection. The post then sets the systematic reports aside in favor of that feed, which is the named failure.
Contrast: The systematic reports exist for both measures. Police-recorded: the FBI estimates 5,245,768 property crimes in 2025, 1,534.8 per 100,000, down from 1,761.1 in 2024 (FBI, *UCR Summary of Reported Crimes in the Nation, 2025*, URL above); the 1991 property crime rate was 5,140.2 per 100,000 (FBI, *Crime in the United States, 2010*, Table 1, URL above), and 1,534.8 ÷ 5,140.2 = 0.30. Survey-based, including unreported crime: the violent victimization rate went from 79.8 per 1,000 in 1993 to 23.5 in 2022 (BJS, *Criminal Victimization, 2022*, https://bjs.ojp.gov/library/publications/criminal-victimization-2022) and was 23.3 in 2024 (BJS, *Criminal Victimization, 2024*, URL above). The app feed and the city's counts were not checked in this run.
Next step: name the report behind the million (FBI, *Crime in the United States*, for the stated year) and the source of the city's murder counts. For the break-ins and car thefts, the city police department's burglary and motor vehicle theft counts by year, in place of the feed.

## A stronger version

A version that passed would keep the local claim local: name the city, give its murder counts for a run of years with their source, and state them per 100,000 residents. It would give the national figure with its year, its report, and its rate, next to an earlier year, and drop "ever" unless the long series supports it. For crime that goes unreported it would use the victimization survey, which measures it, in place of a neighborhood feed. And it would say what the added police would cost and what would be given up to pay for them.
