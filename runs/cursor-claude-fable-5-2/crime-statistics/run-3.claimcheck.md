# ClaimCheck — "Crime has never been this bad"

**Source:** `post.md`, read 2026-11-11. Unchanged.
**Central claim:** > "Crime is out of control. It is the worst it has ever been in this country"
**Summary:** clear failures: 3 · partial: 3 · passed: 0 · N/A: 1
**Score:** 1.5 / 6 (1 N/A)

The problem sits in magnitude, boundary, and evidence: a raw count with
no rate or earlier year beside it, one city's one-year change carried to
"everywhere," and no named source for any figure. A legitimate concern
survives: a rise from 30 to 40 murders in one city is worth checking,
and under-reporting to police is real. The post names no country and no
year; the checks below use United States sources and read "last year"
as 2025, and both readings are assumptions. This is a literacy review.
It does not rule the claim true or false.

## Claims read

- **C-1** (central) > "Crime is out of control. It is the worst it has ever been in this country"
- **C-2** > "More than a million violent crimes were reported last year."
- **C-3** > "In my city, murders went from 30 to 40 in a single year. That is a 33 percent jump"
- **C-4** > "what is happening here is happening everywhere."
- **C-5** > "My neighborhood app shows a new break-in or car theft every single day. A few years ago I never saw any of that."
- **C-6** > "the official numbers can't be trusted anyway, because most crime never gets reported."
- **C-7** (side claim) > "The only answer is more police on every street, whatever it costs."

## Findings

### 1 · Mechanistic literacy — partial

> "My neighborhood app shows a new break-in or car theft every single day. A few years ago I never saw any of that." · "the official numbers can't be trusted anyway, because most crime never gets reported."

Claims: C-2, C-5, C-6
Why: Right in one place and wrong in another. C-2 says "reported," and
C-6 is right that police figures count crime known to police, not all
crime. But C-5 reads posts seen in an app as crimes that occurred, and
C-6 treats under-reporting as a reason to set the numbers aside when it
bears on the level of crime, not on which way crime is moving.
Contrast: BJS, *Criminal Victimization, 2024*: 23.3 violent
victimizations per 1,000 persons age 12 or older in 2024, of which 11.2
per 1,000 were reported to police. 11.2 / 23.3 = 48.1% reported, so
51.9% were not
(https://bjs.ojp.gov/library/publications/criminal-victimization-2024).
That survey exists to count crime "reported and not reported to
police." Its 2025 preliminary edition puts violent crime excluding
simple assault at 9.9 per 1,000 in 2024 and 7.4 per 1,000 in 2025, a
25% decline ((9.9 − 7.4) / 9.9 = 25.3%)
(https://bjs.ojp.gov/library/publications/preliminary-victimization-estimates-ncvs-2025).
The app's posting volume and membership were not checked in this run.
Next step: BJS, *Criminal Victimization, 2024*, tables on victimizations
reported to police by type of crime, for the property-crime share. For
C-5, the city police department's incident data for burglary and motor
vehicle theft over the same years as the app comparison.

### 2 · Quantitative and comparative literacy — clear failure

> "More than a million violent crimes were reported last year. A million."

Claims: C-1, C-2
Why: An absolute number with no denominator and no earlier year beside
it, used to support "the worst it has ever been." A claim about "ever"
needs a rate and a series; the post gives neither. C-3 does state its
baseline (30 to 40, and (40 − 30) / 30 = 33.3% is correct), but gives no
population and no earlier years.
Contrast: FBI, *UCR Summary of Reported Crimes in the Nation, 2025*
(released August 2026): an estimated 1,119,768 violent crime offenses in
2025, a rate of 327.6 per 100,000 inhabitants, down from 362.9 in 2024
(https://cde.ucr.cjis.gov/LATEST/resources/reports/UCR_Summary_of_Reported_Crimes_in_the_Nation_2025.pdf).
100,000 / 327.6 = about 1 offense per 305 inhabitants. Comparisons from
FBI, *Crime in the United States, 2019*, Table 1
(https://ucr.fbi.gov/crime-in-the-u.s/2019/crime-in-the-u.s.-2019/topic-pages/tables/table-1):
2000: 1,425,486 offenses, rate 506.5; 2014: 1,153,022 offenses, rate
361.6, the lowest rate in that 2000–2019 table; 2019: 1,203,808
offenses, rate 366.7. The 2025 rate is (506.5 − 327.6) / 506.5 = 35.3%
below 2000 and (361.6 − 327.6) / 361.6 = 9.4% below 2014. Every year in
that table also exceeds a million offenses, so "more than a million"
does not separate a high year from a low one. The 2000–2019 totals use
the legacy definition of rape (table footnote 2); which definition the
2025 estimate uses was not checked, so the cross-series comparison is
approximate. Years before 2000 were not checked in this run.
Next step: FBI Crime Data Explorer, national violent crime rate per
100,000 for every available year, including years before 2000. Look for
the highest year and where the latest year sits against it.

### 3 · Scale and boundary literacy — clear failure

> "In my city, murders went from 30 to 40 in a single year. That is a 33 percent jump, and what is happening here is happening everywhere."

Claims: C-1, C-3, C-4, C-5
Why: One city's one-year change is carried to the whole country, and a
neighborhood feed is offered beside it as further support. The central
claim is national and all-time; the evidence is one city, one
neighborhood, and one year. The conclusion rests on that step.
Contrast: FBI press release, August 14, 2026: nationally, murder and
nonnegligent manslaughter offenses "decreased an estimated 18.1%" from
2024 to 2025, and violent crime decreased 9.3%
(https://www.fbi.gov/news/press-releases/fbi-releases-2025-reported-crimes-in-the-nation-statistics).
A national decline does not show the city's count is wrong: a city can
rise while the national total falls. The city is not named, so its
figures were not checked in this run.
Next step: the city police department's homicide counts, or the FBI
Crime Data Explorer agency page for that department, for at least ten
years, with the city's population for a rate. Look for whether 30 to 40
is outside the usual year-to-year swing.

### 6 · Recency and updating literacy — partial

> "last year" · "A few years ago I never saw any of that."

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-5
Why: Right but unstated where it matters. No figure carries a year and
the post itself is undated, so a reader cannot tell which edition any
number comes from. The one national figure is not superseded: "more
than a million" agrees with the latest edition, if "last year" is 2025.
Contrast: The latest editions are FBI, *Reported Crimes in the Nation,
2025*, released August 14, 2026 (1,119,768 estimated violent crime
offenses), and BJS, *Preliminary Victimization Estimates from the NCVS,
2025*, published October 26, 2026 (7.5 million violent victimizations,
25.7 per 1,000 persons age 12 or older; final estimates "forthcoming in
2027"). The FBI summary also previews January–June 2026 against
January–June 2025: reported violent crime offenses down 10.6%,
preliminary (URLs under **Sources opened**).
Next step: put a year on each figure, then confirm against the FBI
release for that year. For C-3, the city's current-year count to date.

### 7 · Source and evidentiary literacy — clear failure

> "My neighborhood app shows a new break-in or car theft every single day." · "the official numbers can't be trusted anyway"

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-5, C-6
Why: No figure is traced to a source a reader can open. An app feed and

those reports aside while C-2 appears to quote one of them.
Contrast: The systematic reports exist and are public: the FBI's annual
estimates of offenses known to law enforcement, and the BJS National
Crime Victimization Survey, which covers crime "reported and not
reported to police" (URLs under **Sources opened**).
Next step: name the report behind C-2 (FBI, *Reported Crimes in the
Nation*, and the year), the police department release behind C-3, and
BJS *Criminal Victimization* for C-6.

## A stronger version

A stronger version would stay inside the boundary its evidence covers:
name the city and the year, give murders as a rate per 100,000 beside
the count, and show enough earlier years to tell a one-year swing from
a trend. A national claim would need the national rate series rather
than one year's count, and "worst ever" would need the highest year
named. The under-reporting point would cite the victimization survey
that measures unreported crime and say what that survey shows about
direction. The app observation would be offered as what prompted the
question, with police incident data as the measure. The remedy would
name its cost and what it would be paid from.
