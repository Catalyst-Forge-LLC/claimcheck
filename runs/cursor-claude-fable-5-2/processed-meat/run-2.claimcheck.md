# ClaimCheck — Bacon is the new cigarettes

**Source:** `post.md`, read 2026-11-11. Unchanged.
**Central claim:** > "A ham sandwich is as dangerous as smoking."
**Summary:** clear failures: 2 · partial: 5 · passed: 0 · N/A: 0
**Score:** 2.5 / 7 (1 unverified)

The problem sits in dimensions 1 and 2. The post reads a shared
evidence category as equal danger, and it reads an 18 percent relative
increase as "nearly one in five people." The other five dimensions are
partial: the post quotes the agency's classification and its
50-gram figure accurately, then drops the dose, the date, and the
document. A legitimate concern survives: the agency the post names did
conclude in 2015 that processed meat causes colorectal cancer and that
the risk rises with the amount eaten. This is a literacy review. It
does not rule the claim true or false.

## Claims read

- **C-1** > "the World Health Organization has put bacon, ham, and hot dogs in the same cancer category as cigarettes and asbestos"
- **C-2** (central) > "A ham sandwich is as dangerous as smoking."
- **C-3** > "Just 50 grams a day raises your risk of bowel cancer by 18 percent."
- **C-4** > "That means nearly one in five people who eat the stuff will get cancer from it."
- **C-5** > "My uncle had bacon every morning of his life and died of colon cancer at 61. I don't need a study to tell me why."
- **C-6** > "There is no safe amount."
- **C-7** (side claim) > "We threw out everything in the fridge and switched to turkey bacon and chicken sausages, and you should too."

## Findings

### 1 · Mechanistic literacy — clear failure

> "in the same cancer category as cigarettes and asbestos" · "A ham sandwich is as dangerous as smoking."

Claims: C-1, C-2
Why: Treats hazard and risk as one thing. C-1 reports the category
correctly; C-2 then reads it as a measure of how dangerous the food
is. The category ranks how strong the evidence is that something can
cause cancer, not how much cancer it causes.
Contrast: IARC Q&A (2015), on whether processed meat is as
carcinogenic as tobacco smoking and asbestos: "No, processed meat has
been classified in the same category as causes of cancer such as
tobacco smoking and asbestos (IARC Group 1, carcinogenic to humans),
but this does NOT mean that they are all equally dangerous. The IARC
classifications describe the strength of the scientific evidence about
an agent being a cause of cancer, rather than assessing the level of
risk."
<https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/Monographs-QA_Vol114.pdf>.
WHO's own Q&A (page dated 23 September 2026) says the categories
indicate "the strength of the evidence" and do "not measure the
likelihood that cancer will occur."
<https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/cancer-carcinogenicity-of-the-consumption-of-red-meat-and-processed-meat>
Next step: IARC Monographs Q&A, Volume 114, the question on tobacco
smoking and asbestos. Look for the line that separates strength of
evidence from level of risk.

### 2 · Quantitative and comparative literacy — clear failure

> "raises your risk of bowel cancer by 18 percent. That means nearly one in five people who eat the stuff will get cancer from it."

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-4
Why: C-4 reads a relative increase as an absolute risk. An 18 percent
rise multiplies the risk a person started with, and the post never
gives that starting risk, so "one in five" does not follow from C-3.
The comparison with smoking in C-2 puts no size on either side.
Contrast:
- The figure is relative. IARC Q&A (2015): "every 50 gram portion of
  processed meat eaten daily increases the risk of colorectal cancer
  by about 18%." (URL above.)
- A baseline. NCI SEER: "Approximately 3.9 percent of men and women
  will be diagnosed with colorectal cancer at some point during their
  lifetime, based on 2021–2023 data."
  <https://seer.cancer.gov/statfacts/html/colorect.html>
  Arithmetic: 3.9% × 1.18 = 4.602%, about 4.6%. 4.6% − 3.9% = 0.7
  percentage points, about 7 more people in 1,000 over a lifetime.
  The post's reading, 18% of 1,000, is 180 in 1,000. This is an
  illustration: the U.S. average already includes people who eat
  processed meat, and the post names no country.
- Smoking, by relative risk. CDC (page dated May 18, 2026): "People
  who smoke cigarettes are 15 to 30 times more likely to get lung
  cancer or die from lung cancer than people who do not smoke."
  <https://www.cdc.gov/lung-cancer/risk-factors/index.html>
  Against that, 18 percent is 1.18 times. The two figures are for
  different cancers, each the one its agent is best known for.
- Smoking and two other cases, by deaths. IARC Q&A (2015), citing
  the Global Burden of Disease Project: "about 34 000 cancer deaths
  per year worldwide are attributable to diets high in processed
  meat," against "about 1 million cancer deaths per year globally due
  to tobacco smoking, 600 000 per year due to alcohol consumption, and
  more than 200 000 per year due to air pollution." Arithmetic:
  1,000,000 ÷ 34,000 ≈ 29. These are the estimates IARC quoted in
  2015.
Next step: the lifetime-risk figure for colorectal cancer from the
reader's national cancer registry (for the U.S., NCI SEER Cancer Stat
Facts: Colorectal Cancer). Multiply it by 1.18 and compare the result
with "one in five."

### 3 · Scale and boundary literacy — partial

> "Just 50 grams a day" · "people who eat the stuff" · "A ham sandwich" · "bowel cancer" · "will get cancer"

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-4, C-5
Why: C-3 keeps the boundary of the figure: a daily 50-gram portion
and one cancer. C-4 widens it to anyone who eats any amount and to
cancer in general, C-2 shrinks it to one sandwich, and C-5 applies a
population figure to one person. The central claim would fail on
dimension 1 even with the dose restored, so this does not change the
conclusion.
Contrast: IARC press release No. 240 (26 October 2015): "each 50 gram
portion of processed meat eaten daily increases the risk of colorectal
cancer by 18%," and "For an individual, the risk of developing
colorectal cancer because of their consumption of processed meat
remains small, but this risk increases with the amount of meat
consumed."
<https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/pr240_E.pdf>
On which cancer, IARC Q&A (2015): "eating processed meat causes
colorectal cancer. An association with stomach cancer was also seen,
but the evidence is not conclusive."
Next step: IARC press release No. 240, the paragraph headed "Meat
consumption and its effects." Look for the unit the 18% is tied to
(per 50 grams, eaten daily) and the sentence on individual risk.

### 4 · Trade-off and substitution literacy — partial

> "switched to turkey bacon and chicken sausages, and you should too."

Claims: C-7 (side claim)
Why: The post names its substitute, which is half of what this
dimension asks, but not whether the substitute falls under the same
classification or what the swap costs. The remedy is a side claim, so
the finding is capped at partial. No position is taken here on the
remedy.
Contrast: The classification is defined by process, not by animal.
IARC Q&A (2015): "Processed meat refers to meat that has been
transformed through salting, curing, fermentation, smoking, or other
processes," and "processed meats may also contain other red meats,
poultry, offal, or meat by-products." On choosing between kinds:
"there is not enough information to say whether higher or lower cancer
risks are related to eating any particular type of red meat or
processed meat." On unprocessed poultry: "The cancer risks associated
with consumption of poultry and fish were not evaluated." Whether the
products the post names are cured, salted, or smoked: not checked in
this run.
Next step: the ingredient labels of the turkey bacon and chicken
sausages. Look for curing, salting, or smoking, which is what the IARC
definition turns on.

### 5 · Output and value accounting — partial

Absent: any mention of what the food provides, where the post says it
"threw out everything in the fridge."

Claims: C-7 (side claim)
Why: Counts the cost and leaves out the benefit. The central claim is
about the size of the danger and does not depend on it, so the finding
is capped at partial.
Contrast: IARC Q&A (2015), under "Should I stop eating meat?":
"Eating meat has known health benefits. Many national health
recommendations advise people to limit intake of processed meat and
red meat." WHO Q&A (page dated 23 September 2026): "Meat can be part
of a healthy diet and provides nutrients that are important for good
health." Both sentences are about meat in general, not processed meat
alone.
Next step: IARC Monographs Q&A, Volume 114, "Should I stop eating
meat?" Then the reader's national dietary guideline, for what it says
about processed meat specifically.

### 6 · Recency and updating literacy — partial · unverified

> "It's official: the World Health Organization has put bacon, ham, and hot dogs in the same cancer category"

Absent: a date for the classification or for the 18 percent figure.

Claims: C-1, C-3
Why: The post presents the classification as news and dates nothing.
The classification is from 2015, eleven years before this review
(2026 − 2015 = 11), and it still stands, so the missing date does not
change the conclusion. Whether a later pooled analysis has revised the
18 percent figure was not checked, hence the flag.
Contrast: IARC press release No. 240 is dated 26 October 2015. The
IARC Q&A (2015) attributes the 18% to "An analysis of data from 10
studies." WHO's Q&A page dated 23 September 2026 still states
"Processed meat has been classified as Group 1, carcinogenic to
humans," and adds: "WHO is currently developing a guideline on the
optimal intake of animal-source foods, which will include
recommendations on the amount of red and processed meat to consume."
Later pooled estimates: not checked in this run.
Next step: World Cancer Research Fund / AICR, Continuous Update
Project report on colorectal cancer, the processed meat dose-response
section. Look for the risk increase per 50 grams a day and its year.
Then the WHO guideline on animal-source foods, once published.

### 7 · Source and evidentiary literacy — partial

> "the World Health Organization has put…" · "I don't need a study to tell me why." · "There is no safe amount."

Claims: C-1, C-3, C-5, C-6
Why: Half right. The post names an agency, and its classification and
50-gram figure match what that agency published. It names no document,
date, or link, it sets one case above the studies in so many words
(C-5), and C-6 states as settled what the agency said it could not
conclude.
Contrast: The evaluation was made by "the International Agency for
Research on Cancer (IARC), the cancer agency of the World Health
Organization" (press release No. 240, 2015). It "considered more than
800 studies." On C-6, IARC Q&A (2015): "The risk increases with the
amount of meat consumed, but the data available for evaluation did not
permit a conclusion about whether a safe level exists." WHO's 2026
page repeats that the data "did not permit a conclusion at the time of
the original assessment about whether a safe level exists." On C-5,
one case cannot show cause, and the age is not unusual for this
cancer: NCI SEER reports 22.3% of new colorectal cancer cases
diagnosed at ages 55–64 (2019–2023) and 19.1% of deaths at ages 55–64
(2020–2024).
Next step: IARC Monographs Volume 114, *Red Meat and Processed Meat*,
and its summary in *The Lancet Oncology* (2015). Look for the pooled
analysis behind the 18% and its stated scope.

## A stronger version

A legitimate concern survives. A version that passed the failed
dimensions would say that IARC, the WHO's cancer agency, classified
processed meat in 2015 as a cause of colorectal cancer, and that the
category describes the strength of that evidence, not the size of the
risk. It would give the 18 percent as a relative increase per 50 grams
eaten daily, set beside a named baseline lifetime risk, and it would
size any comparison with smoking in the same units. It would date the
figures, name the document, say that no safe level has been
established rather than that none exists, and say whether the
substitute it recommends is itself cured or smoked.
