# ClaimCheck — Bacon is the new cigarettes

**Source:** `post.md`, read 2026-11-11. Unchanged.
**Central claim:** > "A ham sandwich is as dangerous as smoking."
**Summary:** clear failures: 2 · partial: 5 · passed: 0 · N/A: 0
**Score:** 2.5 / 7

The problem sits in dimensions 1 and 2. The post reads a shared
evidence category as equal danger, and reads an 18 percent relative
increase as 18 percent of eaters getting cancer. The other five are
partial: the post states the dose, the cancer site, and the agency's
figure correctly in one place, then drops them in another. A
legitimate concern survives: IARC (2015) concluded that processed
meat causes colorectal cancer and that the risk rises with the
amount eaten. This is a literacy review. It does not rule the claim
true or false.

## Claims read

- **C-1** > "the World Health Organization has put bacon, ham, and hot dogs in the same cancer category as cigarettes and asbestos"
- **C-2** (central) > "A ham sandwich is as dangerous as smoking."
- **C-3** > "Just 50 grams a day raises your risk of bowel cancer by 18 percent."
- **C-4** > "nearly one in five people who eat the stuff will get cancer from it"
- **C-5** > "My uncle had bacon every morning of his life and died of colon cancer at 61. I don't need a study to tell me why."
- **C-6** > "There is no safe amount."
- **C-7** (side claim) > "We threw out everything in the fridge and switched to turkey bacon and chicken sausages, and you should too."

## Findings

### 1 · Mechanistic literacy — clear failure

> "in the same cancer category as cigarettes and asbestos" · "A ham sandwich is as dangerous as smoking."

Claims: C-1, C-2
Why: Treats hazard and risk as one thing. C-1 states the category
correctly. C-2 then reads a shared category as equal danger. An
IARC group ranks how strong the evidence is that something can
cause cancer, not how much cancer it causes.
Contrast: IARC's Q&A (2015) asks this question directly and answers:
"No, processed meat has been classified in the same category as
causes of cancer such as tobacco smoking and asbestos (IARC Group 1,
carcinogenic to humans), but this does NOT mean that they are all
equally dangerous. The IARC classifications describe the strength of
the scientific evidence about an agent being a cause of cancer,
rather than assessing the level of risk."
https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/Monographs-QA_Vol114.pdf
Next step: IARC Monographs Q&A, Volume 114, the answer on Group 1
and tobacco. Then the IARC Monographs Preamble, for what each group
means.

### 2 · Quantitative and comparative literacy — clear failure

> "raises your risk of bowel cancer by 18 percent" · "nearly one in five people who eat the stuff will get cancer from it"

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-4
Why: A relative increase is read as an absolute risk: 18 percent
more than a baseline becomes 18 percent of people. The post gives no
baseline for bowel cancer, and no figure for smoking, which is the
comparison its central claim makes.
Contrast: Two baselines, with the post's 18 percent applied to each.

| Baseline (lifetime risk of colorectal / bowel cancer) | × 1.18 | Rise |
| --- | --- | --- |
| U.S., about 3.9% (NCI SEER, 2021–2023 data) | 3.9% × 1.18 = 4.6% | 0.7 points |
| UK females born 1961, 1 in 20 (5%) (Cancer Research UK, calculated 2023) | 5% × 1.18 = 5.9% | 0.9 points |
| UK males born 1961, 1 in 17 (6%) (Cancer Research UK, calculated 2023) | 6% × 1.18 = 7.1% | 1.1 points |

The post's "nearly one in five" is about 20%. The table is
illustrative: these population baselines already include people who
eat processed meat.
https://seer.cancer.gov/statfacts/html/colorect.html ·
https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/health-professional/cancer-statistics/statistics-by-cancer-type/bowel-cancer/risk-factors

For the smoking comparison, IARC's Q&A (2015), citing the Global
Burden of Disease Project: "about 34 000 cancer deaths per year
worldwide are attributable to diets high in processed meat", against
"about 1 million cancer deaths per year globally due to tobacco
smoking, 600 000 per year due to alcohol consumption, and more than
200 000 per year due to air pollution." By division: 34,000 ÷
1,000,000 = 3.4% of the tobacco figure; 34,000 ÷ 600,000 = 5.7% of
the alcohol figure; 34,000 ÷ 200,000 = 17% of the air-pollution
figure. IARC's press release (2015): "For an individual, the risk of
developing colorectal cancer because of their consumption of
processed meat remains small, but this risk increases with the
amount of meat consumed".
Next step: the cancer registry's lifetime-risk figure for the
reader's own country, multiplied by 1.18. Then the Global Burden of
Disease results tool (IHME) for current deaths attributed to
processed meat and to tobacco, since IARC's figures are as quoted in
2015.

### 3 · Scale and boundary literacy — partial

> "Just 50 grams a day raises your risk of bowel cancer by 18 percent." · "nearly one in five people who eat the stuff will get cancer from it" · "There is no safe amount."

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-4, C-6
Why: Right in one place and wrong in another. C-3 keeps the figure's
boundary: a daily 50 gram portion and one cancer site. C-4 and C-6
then drop both, carrying it to anyone "who eat[s] the stuff", to any
amount, and to "cancer" without a site. C-2 applies it to a single
sandwich.
Contrast: IARC's Q&A (2015): "every 50 gram portion of processed
meat eaten daily increases the risk of colorectal cancer by about
18%." On a safe amount it says: "The risk increases with the amount
of meat consumed, but the data available for evaluation did not
permit a conclusion about whether a safe level exists." WHO's Q&A
page (dated 23 September 2026) says the same of the data "at the
time of the original assessment" and adds that WHO "is currently
developing a guideline on the optimal intake of animal-source
foods". The meta-analysis behind the figure (Chan et
al., 2011) reports a relative risk of 1.18 per 50 g/day (95% CI
1.10–1.28, 9 studies) for colorectal cancer; for rectal cancer alone
"no significant dose-response association was observed" (1.12, 95%
CI 0.99–1.28). The studies' highest intake groups averaged "from 16
to 122 grams per day".
https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/cancer-carcinogenicity-of-the-consumption-of-red-meat-and-processed-meat ·
https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0020456
Next step: Chan et al. 2011, Table 1 and Figure 5, for the range of
intakes the studies cover and the shape of the dose-response curve.
WHO's guideline on animal-source foods, when it is published.

### 4 · Trade-off and substitution literacy — partial

> "switched to turkey bacon and chicken sausages, and you should too"

Claims: C-7 (side claim)
Why: Names the substitute. Does not say whether the substitute
leaves the category the post objects to, or what discarding
"everything in the fridge" costs. The central claim does not depend
on the remedy, so the finding is capped at partial. No position
taken on the swap.
Contrast: IARC defines processed meat by the process, not the
animal: "meat that has been transformed through salting, curing,
fermentation, smoking, or other processes to enhance flavour or
improve preservation. Most processed meats contain pork or beef, but
processed meats may also contain other red meats, poultry, offal".
Asked "Should we eat only poultry and fish?", the same Q&A (2015)
answers: "The cancer risks associated with consumption of poultry
and fish were not evaluated." A later meta-analysis (Vieira et al.,
2017, abstract) found intakes of "poultry" among foods "not
associated with colorectal cancer risk"; that is poultry intake
overall, not cured poultry products. How any given turkey bacon or
chicken sausage is made: not checked in this run.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28407090/
Next step: the product labels (cured, smoked, salted, nitrite or
nitrate added) against the definition of processed meat in IARC
Monographs Volume 114.

### 5 · Output and value accounting — partial

Absent: any mention of what the food provides, where the post says "We threw out everything in the fridge".

Claims: C-7 (side claim)
Why: Counts the cost and names no benefit. The central claim stands
or falls without it, so this is partial.
Contrast: IARC's Q&A (2015), under "Should I stop eating meat?":
"Eating meat has known health benefits. Many national health
recommendations advise people to limit intake of processed meat and
red meat, which are linked to increased risks of death from heart
disease, diabetes, and other illnesses." IARC's press release (2015)
says the results let governments "balance the risks and benefits of
eating red meat and processed meat".
https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/pr240_E.pdf
Next step: IARC Monographs Q&A, Volume 114, "Should I stop eating
meat?". Then the national dietary guideline for the reader's
country, for what it says about processed meat's place in a diet.

### 6 · Recency and updating literacy — partial

> "It's official: the World Health Organization has put bacon, ham, and hot dogs in the same cancer category"

Claims: C-1, C-3
Why: Right but unstated where it matters. No figure in the post
carries a date, and "It's official" reads as news. The
classification dates from 2015 and the 18 percent from a
meta-analysis published in 2011. Nothing opened in this run shows
either one replaced, so the missing dates do not change the
conclusion.
Contrast: IARC's press release No. 240 is dated 26 October 2015.
Chan et al. was published 6 June 2011, with studies "identified in
PubMed until March 2011". WHO's Q&A page dated 23 September 2026
still carries the question "Processed meat has been classified as
Group 1, carcinogenic to humans. What does this mean?" and answers
it in the present tense. IARC's classification page (Volumes 1–142,
last update 30 October 2026) cautions: "Significant new information
might support a different classification." A later update of the
same review (Vieira et al., 2017, searches to 31 May 2015) reports
in its abstract a 12% increase "for each 100 g/day increase of red
and processed meat intake"; the abstract gives no figure for
processed meat alone.
https://monographs.iarc.who.int/agents-classified-by-the-iarc/
Next step: IARC-MonDO, the entry for consumption of processed meat,
for the current evaluation and its date. Vieira et al. 2017, full
text, the dose-response result for processed meat alone.

### 7 · Source and evidentiary literacy — partial

> "the World Health Organization has put bacon, ham, and hot dogs in the same cancer category" · "My uncle had bacon every morning of his life and died of colon cancer at 61. I don't need a study to tell me why."

Claims: C-1, C-3, C-4, C-5, C-6
Why: Half right. The post names the agency, and C-3 matches the
agency's own wording, so a reader can find the trail. It names no
report, date, or link. C-5 then sets one case above the studies,
and C-4 and C-6 appear in none of the sources opened.
Contrast: The classification is from IARC, "the cancer agency of the
World Health Organization" (press release, 2015), to be published as
Volume 114 of the IARC Monographs; its Working Group "considered
more than 800 studies". The 18 percent comes from "an analysis of
data from 10 studies" (IARC Q&A, 2015). On a single case: Cancer
Research UK's page states "13% of bowel cancer cases in the UK are
caused by eating processed meat" (year not given in the text
retrieved); by subtraction, 100% − 13% = 87% are attributed to
something else.
https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/pr240_E.pdf ·
https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/health-professional/cancer-statistics/statistics-by-cancer-type/bowel-cancer/risk-factors
Next step: IARC Monographs Volume 114, and the summary the press
release points to in *The Lancet Oncology* (2015), for the
evaluation and its stated scope.

## A stronger version

The concern holds without the comparison to smoking. A version that
passes would say that IARC, WHO's cancer agency, classified
processed meat in 2015 as a cause of colorectal cancer, and that the
group states how sure the evidence is, not how large the risk is. It
would give the 18 percent as a relative increase per 50 grams eaten
daily and put it on a named baseline for the reader's country. It
would say that risk rises with the amount and that no safe level has
been established either way. It would say whether the substitute it
recommends is itself cured or smoked, and name the report it relies
on.
