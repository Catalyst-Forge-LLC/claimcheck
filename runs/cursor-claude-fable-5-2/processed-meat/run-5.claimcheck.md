# ClaimCheck — Bacon is the new cigarettes

**Source:** `post.md`, read 11 November 2026. Unchanged.
**Central claim:** > "A ham sandwich is as dangerous as smoking."
**Summary:** clear failures: 2 · partial: 5 · passed: 0 · N/A: 0
**Score:** 2.5 / 7

The problem sits in dimensions 1 and 2: the post reads a shared evidence category as equal danger, and it reads an 18 percent relative increase as 18 percent of eaters getting cancer. The other five dimensions are partial: the dose and the cancer site are stated once and then dropped, the swap to poultry products is not checked against the category it leaves, the food's benefit goes unmentioned, nothing is dated, and one family case is set above studies. A legitimate concern survives: IARC concluded in 2015 that eating processed meat causes colorectal cancer and that the risk rises with the amount eaten. This is a literacy review and does not rule the claim true or false.

## Claims read

- **C-1** > "the World Health Organization has put bacon, ham, and hot dogs in the same cancer category as cigarettes and asbestos."
- **C-2** (central) > "A ham sandwich is as dangerous as smoking."
- **C-3** > "Just 50 grams a day raises your risk of bowel cancer by 18 percent."
- **C-4** > "That means nearly one in five people who eat the stuff will get cancer from it."
- **C-5** > "My uncle had bacon every morning of his life and died of colon cancer at 61. I don't need a study to tell me why."
- **C-6** > "There is no safe amount."
- **C-7** (side claim) > "We threw out everything in the fridge and switched to turkey bacon and chicken sausages, and you should too."

## Findings

### 1 · Mechanistic literacy — clear failure

> "in the same cancer category as cigarettes and asbestos" · "A ham sandwich is as dangerous as smoking."

Claims: C-1, C-2
Why: Treats hazard and risk as one thing. C-1 reports the category correctly; C-2 then reads the category as a measure of danger. An IARC group ranks how strong the evidence is that something can cause cancer, not how much cancer it causes, and the central claim rests on that step.
Contrast: IARC Monographs Q&A, Volume 114 (2015): processed meat "has been classified in the same category as causes of cancer such as tobacco smoking and asbestos (IARC Group 1, carcinogenic to humans), but this does NOT mean that they are all equally dangerous. The IARC classifications describe the strength of the scientific evidence about an agent being a cause of cancer, rather than assessing the level of risk." https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/Monographs-QA_Vol114.pdf. WHO's Q&A page dated 23 September 2026 says the categories indicate "the strength of the evidence as to whether a substance is capable of causing cancer (technically called 'hazard assessment')" and do "not measure the likelihood that cancer will occur (technically called 'risk characterization')". https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/cancer-carcinogenicity-of-the-consumption-of-red-meat-and-processed-meat
Next step: IARC Monographs Q&A, Volume 114, the question on tobacco smoking and asbestos. Look for what a Group 1 listing does and does not say about the size of the effect.

### 2 · Quantitative and comparative literacy — clear failure

> "raises your risk of bowel cancer by 18 percent. That means nearly one in five people who eat the stuff will get cancer from it."

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-4
Why: A relative increase read as an absolute risk, with no baseline. The 18 percent multiplies whatever risk a person already has; it is not the share of eaters who get cancer. The comparison with smoking in C-2 carries no number at all.
Contrast: IARC Q&A, Volume 114 (2015): "every 50 gram portion of processed meat eaten daily increases the risk of colorectal cancer by about 18%" (link above). IARC press release No. 240 (2015): "For an individual, the risk of developing colorectal cancer because of their consumption of processed meat remains small." https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/pr240_E.pdf. Baseline: NCI SEER puts U.S. lifetime risk of a colorectal cancer diagnosis at "approximately 3.9 percent," based on 2021–2023 data. https://seer.cancer.gov/statfacts/html/colorect.html. Arithmetic: 3.9% × 1.18 = 4.602%, about 4.6%; 4.602% − 3.9% = 0.702 percentage points, about 7 more people per 1,000. The post's reading is 18%, or 180 per 1,000. This is an illustration, not an estimate: 3.9% is a U.S. figure for the whole population, which already includes people who eat processed meat. Against smoking, two comparisons. IARC's Q&A (2015), citing the Global Burden of Disease Project: about 34 000 cancer deaths a year worldwide attributable to diets high in processed meat, against about 1 million from tobacco smoking, 600 000 from alcohol, and more than 200 000 from air pollution; 34,000 ÷ 1,000,000 = 3.4% of the tobacco figure. CDC (page dated 18 May 2026): "People who smoke cigarettes are 15 to 30 times more likely to get lung cancer or die from lung cancer than people who do not smoke"; an 18% increase is 1.18 times. Those two ratios are for different cancers. https://www.cdc.gov/lung-cancer/risk-factors/index.html
Next step: the national cancer registry's lifetime-risk figure for colorectal cancer in the reader's country (for the U.S., NCI SEER Cancer Stat Facts: Colorectal Cancer, "Lifetime Risk of Developing Cancer"). Apply the 18% to that baseline.

### 3 · Scale and boundary literacy — partial

> "Just 50 grams a day raises your risk of bowel cancer by 18 percent." · "people who eat the stuff will get cancer from it" · "A ham sandwich"

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-4
Why: Right in one place and wrong in the next. C-3 keeps the figure's boundary: a 50 gram portion eaten every day, and one cancer site. C-4 and C-2 drop both, widening the figure to anyone who eats any amount, to cancer in general, and to a single sandwich.
Contrast: IARC Q&A, Volume 114 (2015): the 18% is for "every 50 gram portion of processed meat eaten daily"; "the risk generally increased with the amount of meat consumed"; the Working Group "concluded that eating processed meat causes colorectal cancer. An association with stomach cancer was also seen, but the evidence is not conclusive." (link above)
Next step: IARC Q&A, Volume 114, "Could you quantify the risk of eating red meat and processed meat?" Look for the dose the figure is tied to and the cancer site it covers.

### 4 · Trade-off and substitution literacy — partial

> "switched to turkey bacon and chicken sausages, and you should too."

Claims: C-7 (side claim)
Why: Names the substitute, which is half the test, but not its cost or whether it sits inside the category the post is leaving. IARC defines processed meat by how it is preserved, not by the animal. The central claim does not depend on the remedy, so the finding is capped at partial. This takes no position on the remedy.
Contrast: IARC Q&A, Volume 114 (2015): "Processed meat refers to meat that has been transformed through salting, curing, fermentation, smoking, or other processes to enhance flavour or improve preservation. Most processed meats contain pork or beef, but processed meats may also contain other red meats, poultry, offal, or meat by-products such as blood." Same document, on eating only poultry and fish: "The cancer risks associated with consumption of poultry and fish were not evaluated." (link above) Whether the products the post names are cured, smoked, or salted was not checked in this run.
Next step: the ingredient and processing statement on the turkey bacon and chicken sausage labels, read against IARC's definition of processed meat.

### 5 · Output and value accounting — partial

> Absent: any mention of what the food provides, where the post says "We threw out everything in the fridge".

Claims: C-7 (side claim)
Why: Counts the cost and leaves out the benefit. The post raises the topic by calling for the food to be thrown out, and the sources it leans on both name a benefit. The central claim does not depend on it.
Contrast: IARC Q&A, Volume 114 (2015), "Should I stop eating meat?": "Eating meat has known health benefits. Many national health recommendations advise people to limit intake of processed meat and red meat" (link above). WHO Q&A page (23 September 2026): "Meat can be part of a healthy diet and provides nutrients that are important for good health." (link above)
Next step: IARC Monographs Q&A, Volume 114, "Should I stop eating meat?"

### 6 · Recency and updating literacy — partial

> "It's official: the World Health Organization has put bacon, ham, and hot dogs in the same cancer category" · Absent: a date for the classification or for the 18 percent figure.

Claims: C-1, C-3
Why: Presents an evaluation announced on 26 October 2015 as news and dates neither figure. The classification still stands and a later systematic estimate is close to the post's, so the age does not change the conclusion: partial, not clear failure.
Contrast: IARC press release No. 240 is dated 26 October 2015 (link above). WHO's Q&A page dated 23 September 2026 still lists processed meat as Group 1 and adds: "WHO is currently developing a guideline on the optimal intake of animal-source foods, which will include recommendations on the amount of red and processed meat to consume." (link above) The text of that page as retrieved does not restate the 18% figure. WCRF/AICR, *Diet, nutrition, physical activity and colorectal cancer* (2017, revised 2018), section 7.5.3: "a 16 per cent increased risk per 50 grams of processed meat per day (RR 1.16 (95% CI 1.08–1.26)", and "The CUP findings are similar to the result from the 2010 SLR (RR 1.18 (95% CI 1.10–1.28))." https://www.aicr.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/colorectal-cancer-2017-report.pdf. With that figure, 3.9% × 1.16 = 4.524%, about 4.5%.
Next step: WHO's guideline on animal-source foods when it is published; until then, the WCRF/AICR colorectal cancer report, section 7.5.3, for the current dose-response figure.

### 7 · Source and evidentiary literacy — partial

> "the World Health Organization has put bacon, ham, and hot dogs in the same cancer category" · "I don't need a study to tell me why." · "There is no safe amount."

Claims: C-1, C-3, C-5, C-6
Why: Right in one place and wrong in another. C-1 and C-3 trace to a systematic evaluation and match it, though the post names only the organization and no document. C-5 sets one case above studies in so many words, and C-6 states as settled what the source leaves open.
Contrast: IARC press release No. 240 (2015) carries both of the post's figures: Group 1, and "each 50 gram portion of processed meat eaten daily increases the risk of colorectal cancer by 18%" (link above). On C-6, IARC Q&A, Volume 114 (2015): "the data available for evaluation did not permit a conclusion about whether a safe level exists"; WHO's 23 September 2026 page says the same. On C-5, NCI SEER: about 3.9 percent of U.S. men and women are diagnosed with colorectal cancer in their lifetime (2021–2023 data), and 19.1% of U.S. colorectal cancer deaths occur at ages 55–64 (2020–2024). Those are counts for a whole population; neither they nor one case can show what caused that case. (links above)
Next step: the summary of the evaluation in *The Lancet Oncology* that IARC's press release No. 240 points to, and IARC Monographs Volume 114. Look for the meta-analysis behind the 18%, its confidence interval, and what the Working Group says about a safe level.

## Unverified

No finding's status depends on these.

- Which meta-analysis IARC's 18% comes from, and its confidence interval — the IARC Q&A says only "an analysis of data from 10 studies"; the *Lancet Oncology* summary and Monograph Volume 114 were not opened — check there. WCRF/AICR reports the same value, RR 1.18, for its 2010 review; whether it is the same analysis was not confirmed.
- Whether the turkey bacon and chicken sausages the post names are cured, smoked, or salted — the post does not name products — check the product labels.
- Whether a pooled estimate newer than the WCRF/AICR 2018 revision exists — not searched beyond that report — check WCRF's Continuous Update Project and the systematic reviews for WHO's forthcoming guideline.
- The year of the Global Burden of Disease estimates behind the 34 000 and 1 million figures, and their current values — the IARC Q&A (2015) calls them "the most recent estimates" without a year — check the Global Burden of Disease results from IHME.
- Lifetime risk of colorectal cancer outside the United States — the post names no country; only the U.S. registry was opened — check the national cancer registry concerned.
- The cause of the uncle's cancer in C-5 — a single case cannot be checked against any source.

## A stronger version

A legitimate concern survives. A version that passes would say that Group 1 ranks the strength of the evidence and not the size of the effect; give the 18% as a relative increase per 50 grams eaten daily, on a stated baseline (in the U.S., from about 3.9% to about 4.6% lifetime risk of colorectal cancer); keep the dose and the cancer site attached to the figure; name and date the IARC evaluation (2015); say that no safe level has been established rather than that none exists; check whether the replacement products fall under the same definition; and leave the family case as the reason for the concern, not the proof of it.
