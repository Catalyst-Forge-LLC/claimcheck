# ClaimCheck — Bacon is the new cigarettes

**Source:** `post.md`, read 2026-11-11. Unchanged.
**Central claim:** > "A ham sandwich is as dangerous as smoking."
**Summary:** clear failures: 2 · partial: 5 · passed: 0 · N/A: 0
**Score:** 2.5 / 7

The problem sits in dimensions 1 and 2: the post reads a shared
evidence category as equal danger, and reads an 18 percent relative
increase as a one-in-five chance of cancer. The other five dimensions
are half right: the agency and the 18 percent figure are real, and
the post names a substitute, but dose, dates, benefits, and the
source's own stated limits are left out. A legitimate concern
survives: IARC (2015) and WHO (2026) both say processed meat causes
colorectal cancer and that the risk rises with the amount eaten.
This is a literacy review. It does not rule the claim true or false.

## Claims read

- **C-1** > "the World Health Organization has put bacon, ham, and hot dogs in the same cancer category as cigarettes and asbestos"
- **C-2** (central) > "A ham sandwich is as dangerous as smoking."
- **C-3** > "Just 50 grams a day raises your risk of bowel cancer by 18 percent."
- **C-4** > "That means nearly one in five people who eat the stuff will get cancer from it."
- **C-5** > "My uncle had bacon every morning of his life and died of colon cancer at 61. I don't need a study to tell me why."
- **C-6** (side claim) > "There is no safe amount."
- **C-7** (side claim) > "We threw out everything in the fridge and switched to turkey bacon and chicken sausages, and you should too."

## Findings

### 1 · Mechanistic literacy — clear failure

> "in the same cancer category as cigarettes and asbestos" · "A ham sandwich is as dangerous as smoking."

Claims: C-1, C-2
Why: Treats hazard and risk as one thing. C-1 is accurate as far as
it goes: processed meat, tobacco smoking, and asbestos are all in
IARC Group 1. C-2 then reads the group as a ranking of danger, when
the group ranks how strong the evidence is that something can cause
cancer.
Contrast: IARC Q&A (2015), asked whether Group 1 means processed
meat "is as carcinogenic as tobacco smoking and asbestos": "No,
processed meat has been classified in the same category as causes of
cancer such as tobacco smoking and asbestos (IARC Group 1,
carcinogenic to humans), but this does NOT mean that they are all
equally dangerous. The IARC classifications describe the strength of
the scientific evidence about an agent being a cause of cancer,
rather than assessing the level of risk."
(https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/Monographs-QA_Vol114.pdf)
WHO Q&A (23 September 2026): the categories "indicate the strength of
the evidence as to whether a substance is capable of causing cancer
(technically called 'hazard assessment'), but it does not measure the
likelihood that cancer will occur (technically called 'risk
characterization') at a particular level of exposure to the
substance."
(https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/cancer-carcinogenicity-of-the-consumption-of-red-meat-and-processed-meat)
Next step: IARC Monographs Q&A, Volume 114, the question on tobacco
smoking and asbestos. Look for the sentence that separates strength
of evidence from level of risk.

### 2 · Quantitative and comparative literacy — clear failure

> "raises your risk of bowel cancer by 18 percent" · "nearly one in five people who eat the stuff will get cancer from it"

Claims: C-3, C-4
Why: A relative increase read as an absolute risk, with no baseline.
"18 percent" multiplies whatever the risk already was; it is not the
share of eaters who get cancer. The comparison to smoking is also
given with no size on either side.
Contrast: SEER Cancer Stat Facts: "Approximately 3.9 percent of men
and women will be diagnosed with colorectal cancer at some point
during their lifetime, based on 2021–2023 data" (U.S.).
(https://seer.cancer.gov/statfacts/html/colorect.html)
Arithmetic: 3.9% × 1.18 = 4.602%, about 4.6%. That is 4.6 − 3.9 = 0.7
percentage points, or about 7 more people in 1,000, against the
post's "nearly one in five" (about 200 in 1,000). The 3.9% is an
all-population figure that already includes people who eat processed
meat, so this is an illustration of scale, not the risk of a
non-eater.
Two comparisons with smoking: (a) IARC Q&A (2015), citing the Global
Burden of Disease Project: "about 34 000 cancer deaths per year
worldwide are attributable to diets high in processed meat," against
"about 1 million cancer deaths per year globally due to tobacco
smoking, 600 000 per year due to alcohol consumption, and more than
200 000 per year due to air pollution." 1,000,000 ÷ 34,000 ≈ 29.
(b) CDC (page dated May 18, 2026): "People who smoke cigarettes are
15 to 30 times more likely to get lung cancer or die from lung
cancer than people who do not smoke," against 1.18 times for
colorectal cancer per 50 g of processed meat a day. These are
different cancers with different baselines, so the ratio shows order
of magnitude only.
(https://www.cdc.gov/lung-cancer/risk-factors/index.html)
Next step: the national cancer registry's lifetime-risk figure for
colorectal cancer in the reader's country, then a cohort study that
reports absolute risk in high and low consumers.

### 3 · Scale and boundary literacy — partial

> "Just 50 grams a day raises your risk of bowel cancer" · "people who eat the stuff will get cancer" · "A ham sandwich"

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-4
Why: Right in one place and wrong in another. C-3 keeps the
boundary the figure carries: a 50 gram portion, eaten every day, and
one cancer site. C-4 drops all three ("people who eat the stuff,"
any amount; "cancer," any site), and C-2 sets one sandwich against a
habit.
Contrast: IARC Q&A (2015): "every 50 gram portion of processed meat
eaten daily increases the risk of colorectal cancer by about 18%,"
and "the risk generally increased with the amount of meat consumed."
IARC press release 240 (26 October 2015), Dr Kurt Straif: "For an
individual, the risk of developing colorectal cancer because of
their consumption of processed meat remains small, but this risk
increases with the amount of meat consumed."
(https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/pr240_E.pdf)
AICR (20 September 2017) sizes the portion as "every 50 grams
consumed daily — about one hot dog."
(https://www.aicr.org/news/processed-meats-increase-colorectal-cancer-risk-new-report/)
Next step: WCRF/AICR, *Diet, nutrition, physical activity and
colorectal cancer* (2017, revised 2018), section 7.5.3 and Figure 8,
for the per-50-g dose-response; a national dietary survey for how
many grams a day people actually eat.

### 4 · Trade-off and substitution literacy — partial

> "switched to turkey bacon and chicken sausages, and you should too"

Claims: C-7 (side claim)
Why: Names the substitute, which is half the job, but does not ask
what the swap costs or whether the substitute falls in the same
category, which is defined by how meat is preserved and not by the
animal. The central claim does not depend on the remedy, so the
finding is capped at partial. No position taken on the remedy.
Contrast: IARC press release 240 (2015): "Processed meat refers to
meat that has been transformed through salting, curing,
fermentation, smoking, or other processes," and "processed meats may
also contain other red meats, poultry, offal, or meat by-products."
IARC Q&A (2015), "Should we eat only poultry and fish?": "The cancer
risks associated with consumption of poultry and fish were not
evaluated." WCRF (undated page) groups "meat, processed meat, and
processed poultry" together when describing dietary patterns.
(https://www.wcrf.org/research-policy/evidence-for-our-recommendations/limit-red-processed-meat/)
Next step: the ingredient label of the substitute products, for
curing, smoking, or salting; IARC Monographs Volume 114, the
definition of processed meat.

### 5 · Output and value accounting — partial

Absent: any mention of what the food provides, where the post says
it "threw out everything in the fridge."

Claims: C-7 (side claim)
Why: Counts the cost and leaves out the benefit. The sources the
post leans on name one. The central claim does not depend on it, so
the finding is capped at partial.
Contrast: IARC Q&A (2015), "Should I stop eating meat?": "Eating meat
has known health benefits." WHO Q&A (23 September 2026): "Meat can be
part of a healthy diet and provides nutrients that are important for
good health." IARC press release 240 (2015), Dr Christopher Wild:
"red meat has nutritional value." These statements cover meat in
general or red meat; none of the pages opened states a benefit
specific to processed meat, and WCRF's goal is "very little, if any,
processed meat."
Next step: IARC Monographs Q&A, Volume 114, "Should I stop eating
meat?"; a national food-composition table for the protein, iron, and
sodium in the specific products.

### 6 · Recency and updating literacy — partial

> "It's official" · "raises your risk of bowel cancer by 18 percent"

Claims: C-1, C-3
Why: Neither figure is dated. The classification and the 18 percent
both come from 26 October 2015, and a later systematic report puts
the same dose-response at 16 percent. The classification still
stands and the newer figure is close, so the error does not change
the conclusion: partial.
Contrast: IARC press release 240 is dated 26 October 2015. WCRF/AICR
colorectal cancer report (2017, revised 2018), section 7.5.3: "a 16
per cent increased risk per 50 grams of processed meat per day (RR
1.16 (95% CI 1.08–1.26))," ten studies, 10,738 cases, and "The CUP
findings are similar to the result from the 2010 SLR (RR 1.18 (95%
CI 1.10–1.28))."
(https://www.aicr.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/colorectal-cancer-2017-report.pdf)
With the newer figure, 3.9% × 1.16 = 4.524%, about 4.5%. WHO's Q&A,
dated 23 September 2026, keeps the Group 1 classification and adds:
"WHO is currently developing a guideline on the optimal intake of
animal-source foods, which will include recommendations on the
amount of red and processed meat to consume."
Next step: the WHO guideline on animal-source foods when it is
published; the WCRF/AICR colorectal report for the current
dose-response figure.

### 7 · Source and evidentiary literacy — partial

> "the World Health Organization has put bacon, ham, and hot dogs in the same cancer category" · "I don't need a study to tell me why." · "There is no safe amount."

Claims: C-1, C-3, C-5, C-6
Why: Half right. The post names the agency, and C-1 and C-3 match
its documents, though it cites no report, link, or date. C-5 then
sets one death above the studies in so many words, and C-6 states as
settled something the named agency left open.
Contrast: IARC press release 240 (2015): the Working Group
"considered more than 800 studies"; the 18% comes from "an analysis
of data from 10 studies" (IARC Q&A, 2015). On C-6, IARC Q&A (2015):
"the data available for evaluation did not permit a conclusion about
whether a safe level exists"; WHO (2026) repeats this "at the time
of the original assessment." A different systematic body goes
further: WCRF (undated page) says "no level of intake associated
with a lack of colorectal cancer risk." So C-6 has support the post
does not cite, and not from the source it names. On C-5, SEER:
"Because these statistics are based on large groups of people, they
cannot be used to predict exactly what will happen to an individual
patient," and 19.1% of U.S. colorectal cancer deaths fall at ages
55–64 (2020–2024).
Next step: Bouvard et al., "Carcinogenicity of consumption of red
and processed meat," *The Lancet Oncology* (2015), and IARC
Monographs Volume 114, for the ten-study analysis and its scope.

## A stronger version

A version that passed the two failed dimensions would say that IARC
classified processed meat as a cause of colorectal cancer in 2015 on
the strength of the evidence, and that this places it in the same
evidence group as tobacco without making the two equally dangerous.
It would give the 18 percent (or the later 16 percent) as a relative
increase per 50 grams eaten daily, next to a baseline lifetime risk,
and date both. It would say that the risk rises with the amount
eaten, name what IARC left open about a safe level, and check
whether the proposed substitute is itself cured or smoked.
