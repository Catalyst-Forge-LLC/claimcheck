# ClaimCheck — "Bacon is the new cigarettes"

**Source:** `post.md`, read 11 November 2026. Unchanged.
**Central claim:** > "A ham sandwich is as dangerous as smoking."
**Summary:** clear failures: 2 · partial: 5 · passed: 0 · N/A: 0
**Score:** 2.5 / 7 (1 unverified)

The problem sits in dimensions 1 and 2: the post reads a shared
evidence category as equal danger, and reads an 18 percent relative
increase as an 18 percent chance of cancer. The other five dimensions
are half right: the category and the 18 percent figure both match the
2015 IARC evaluation, but the dose, the date, the substitute, and the
source trail are left loose. A legitimate concern survives, because
IARC found sufficient evidence that processed meat causes colorectal
cancer and that risk rises with the amount eaten. This is a literacy
review and does not rule the claim true or false.

## Claims read

- **C-1** > "the World Health Organization has put bacon, ham, and hot dogs in the same cancer category as cigarettes and asbestos."
- **C-2** (central) > "A ham sandwich is as dangerous as smoking."
- **C-3** > "Just 50 grams a day raises your risk of bowel cancer by 18 percent."
- **C-4** > "That means nearly one in five people who eat the stuff will get cancer from it."
- **C-5** > "My uncle had bacon every morning of his life and died of colon cancer at 61. I don't need a study to tell me why."
- **C-6** > "There is no safe amount."
- **C-7** (side claim) > "We threw out everything in the fridge and switched to turkey bacon and chicken sausages, and you should too."

## Findings

### 1 · Mechanistic literacy — clear failure

> "in the same cancer category as cigarettes and asbestos" · "A ham sandwich is as dangerous as smoking."

Claims: C-1, C-2
Why: Treats hazard and risk as one thing. C-1 states the category
correctly, then C-2 reads a shared category as equal danger. The
category ranks how strong the evidence is that an agent can cause
cancer, not how much cancer it causes, and the central claim rests on
that step.
Contrast: IARC Monographs Q&A, Volume 114 (2015): "processed meat has
been classified in the same category as causes of cancer such as
tobacco smoking and asbestos (IARC Group 1, carcinogenic to humans),
but this does NOT mean that they are all equally dangerous. The IARC
classifications describe the strength of the scientific evidence about
an agent being a cause of cancer, rather than assessing the level of
risk."
https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/Monographs-QA_Vol114.pdf
Next step: IARC Monographs Q&A, Volume 114, the question "Does it mean
that consumption of processed meat is as carcinogenic as tobacco
smoking and asbestos?" Look for the hazard and risk distinction.

### 2 · Quantitative and comparative literacy — clear failure

> "raises your risk of bowel cancer by 18 percent. That means nearly one in five people who eat the stuff will get cancer from it."

Claims: C-3, C-4
Why: A relative increase is read as an absolute risk, and no baseline
is given. Eighteen percent more than a baseline is a different number
from an 18 percent chance, and the size of the gap depends on a
baseline the post never states. The comparison to smoking in C-2 also
carries no number for either side.
Contrast: Two comparisons.
(a) Baseline. NCI SEER puts U.S. lifetime risk of a colorectal cancer
diagnosis at about 3.9 percent (2021–2023 data).
3.9% × 1.18 = 4.602%, about 4.6%. The difference is
4.6% − 3.9% = 0.7 percentage points, about 7 more people in 1,000.
"Nearly one in five" would be close to 200 in 1,000. The 3.9 percent
covers the whole U.S. population, which includes people who eat
processed meat, so this shows the scale and is not an exact estimate.
https://seer.cancer.gov/statfacts/html/colorect.html
(b) Against smoking. The IARC Q&A (2015), citing the Global Burden of
Disease Project, gives about 34,000 cancer deaths per year worldwide
attributable to diets high in processed meat, against about 1 million
per year due to tobacco smoking, 600,000 due to alcohol, and more than
200,000 due to air pollution. 34,000 ÷ 1,000,000 = 3.4%. These are
population totals and depend on how many people are exposed. The same
Q&A describes the processed meat effect as "small increases in the
risk of cancer."
https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/Monographs-QA_Vol114.pdf
Next step: The national cancer registry's lifetime-risk figure for
colorectal cancer in the reader's country (for the U.S., NCI SEER
Cancer Stat Facts: Colorectal Cancer, "Lifetime Risk of Developing
Cancer"). Apply the 18 percent to that figure.

### 3 · Scale and boundary literacy — partial

> "Just 50 grams a day raises your risk of bowel cancer" · "people who eat the stuff will get cancer" · "A ham sandwich"

Claims: C-2, C-3, C-4
Why: C-3 keeps the boundary of the finding: a 50 gram portion eaten
every day, and one cancer site. C-4 then widens it to anyone who eats
any amount and to cancer in general, and C-2 narrows the exposure to a
single sandwich with no amount or frequency. The one-in-five figure
fails mainly on dimension 2, so the boundary slip does not by itself
change the conclusion.
Contrast: IARC press release 240 (26 October 2015): "each 50 gram
portion of processed meat eaten daily increases the risk of colorectal
cancer by 18%." The Q&A adds: "The risk increases with the amount of
meat consumed." The Group 1 finding is for colorectal cancer; for
stomach cancer the Q&A says "the evidence is not conclusive."
https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/pr240_E.pdf
Next step: The Lancet Oncology summary of the evaluation (named in
press release 240) and IARC Monographs Volume 114. Look for the range
of daily intake covered by the 10 studies behind the 18 percent figure.

### 4 · Trade-off and substitution literacy — partial

> "switched to turkey bacon and chicken sausages, and you should too."

Claims: C-7 (side claim)
Why: The post names its substitute, which is half of what this
dimension asks, but not whether the substitute falls outside the
category it is leaving or what the swap costs. The remedy is a side
claim, so the finding is capped at partial. This review takes no
position on the swap.
Contrast: IARC defines processed meat by how it is made, not by the
animal: "meat that has been transformed through salting, curing,
fermentation, smoking, or other processes to enhance flavour or
improve preservation. Most processed meats contain pork or beef, but
processed meats may also contain other red meats, poultry, offal, or
meat by-products such as blood." On unprocessed poultry the Q&A says:
"The cancer risks associated with consumption of poultry and fish were
not evaluated." Whether the products the author bought are cured or
smoked was not checked in this run.
https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/Monographs-QA_Vol114.pdf
Next step: The ingredient label of the turkey bacon and chicken
sausages. Look for curing, smoking, or added nitrite or nitrate, and
compare with the IARC definition above.

### 5 · Output and value accounting — partial

> Absent: any mention of what the food provides, where the post says it "threw out everything in the fridge."

Claims: C-7 (side claim)
Why: Counts the cost and leaves out the benefit. The post objects to a
food and never says what that food supplies to a diet. The central
claim does not depend on it, so the finding is capped at partial.
Contrast: IARC Monographs Q&A, Volume 114 (2015), under "Should I stop
eating meat?": "Eating meat has known health benefits. Many national
health recommendations advise people to limit intake of processed meat
and red meat." That answer is about meat in general, not processed
meat alone. Press release 240 says IARC's results are meant to help
agencies "balance the risks and benefits of eating red meat and
processed meat."
https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/Monographs-QA_Vol114.pdf
Next step: IARC Monographs Q&A, Volume 114, "Should I stop eating
meat?", then the reader's national dietary guideline for its advice on
processed meat.

### 6 · Recency and updating literacy — partial · unverified

> "It's official: the World Health Organization has put bacon, ham, and hot dogs in the same cancer category"

Claims: C-1, C-3
Why: The post gives no date for the classification or the 18 percent
figure and presents both as news, though the evaluation is dated 2015,
eleven years before this reading in 2026. A later systematic estimate
exists and is close to the one quoted, so the missing date does not
change the conclusion. Flagged unverified because the current IARC
list could not be opened to confirm the 2015 classification still
stands.
Contrast: IARC press release 240 is dated 26 October 2015. The World
Cancer Research Fund Third Expert Report (2018), "Meat, fish and dairy
products and the risk of cancer," Table 5.3, gives a relative risk for
colorectal cancer of 1.16 (95% CI 1.08–1.26) per 50 g/day of processed
meat, from 10 studies and 10,738 cases, in a cancer report dated 2017,
and concludes "Convincing: Increases risk." That is 16 percent against
the 18 percent quoted. The table row was read from the excerpt the web
tool returned; the full report was not read in this run.
https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/pr240_E.pdf
https://www.wcrf.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Meat-fish-and-dairy-products.pdf
Next step: IARC Monographs, "List of Classifications," the entry for
processed meat (consumption of). Confirm the group and the volume and
year. Then WCRF 2018, Table 5.3, for the newer dose-response figure.

### 7 · Source and evidentiary literacy — partial

> "the World Health Organization has put bacon, ham, and hot dogs in the same cancer category" · "I don't need a study to tell me why." · "There is no safe amount."

Claims: C-1, C-3, C-5, C-6
Why: Right in one place and wrong in another. C-1 and C-3 trace to a
real systematic evaluation, and the 18 percent per 50 grams matches it,
though the post names only the parent body and gives no report, date,
or link. C-5 then sets one case above that evaluation in so many
words, and C-6 states as settled a point the same evaluation said it
could not settle.
Contrast: IARC press release 240 (2015) describes itself as from "the
cancer agency of the World Health Organization" and reports a Working
Group that "considered more than 800 studies." On a safe amount, the
IARC Q&A (2015) says: "The risk increases with the amount of meat
consumed, but the data available for evaluation did not permit a
conclusion about whether a safe level exists."
https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/pr240_E.pdf
https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/Monographs-QA_Vol114.pdf
Next step: IARC press release 240 and the Q&A for Volume 114, the
question "How much meat is it safe to eat?" Cite the evaluation by
name and date in place of "the World Health Organization."

## A stronger version

A legitimate concern survives. A version that passed the failed
dimensions would say that IARC in 2015 found sufficient evidence that
processed meat causes colorectal cancer, and that this is a statement
about the strength of the evidence, not a ranking next to tobacco. It
would give the 18 percent as a relative increase per 50 grams eaten
daily, beside a named baseline lifetime risk, so the reader can see
the change in absolute terms. It would date the evaluation, name the
report, say that whether a safe level exists was left open, and say
whether the poultry products it switched to are themselves cured or
smoked.
