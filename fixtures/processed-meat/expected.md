Injected: two clear failures, five partials. A health-risk claim.
The underlying finding is real and the report must say so.

Central claim: "A ham sandwich is as dangerous as smoking." Side
claim: the household's swap, "switched to turkey bacon and chicken
sausages, and you should too".

Catch:

1 · mechanism — clear failure. "same cancer category as cigarettes"
   becomes "as dangerous as smoking". Hazard and risk treated as
   one thing: an IARC group states how strong the evidence is that
   something can cause cancer, not how much risk it carries.
2 · magnitude — clear failure. "18 percent" is a relative increase;
   "nearly one in five people who eat the stuff will get cancer"
   reads it as an absolute risk. No baseline risk is given.
3 · boundary — partial. "Just 50 grams a day" carries the dose the
   18% applies to; "people who eat the stuff" drops it, and one
   relative stands in for a population estimate.
4 · tradeoff — partial. The post names what it eats instead, which
   is half the question. Turkey bacon and chicken sausages are
   processed meat under the same definition, so the swap is
   unpriced. It rests on a side claim.
5 · output — partial. The post tells readers to throw the food out
   and never says what the food provides. It raises the topic and
   leaves the benefit out; the central claim does not depend on it.
6 · recency — partial · unverified. The classification is from
   October 2015 and still stands, but the post gives no date and
   presents it as news. The flag is correct when the run did not
   check for a newer estimate than 18% per 50 g; plain `partial`
   is correct when it did.
7 · evidence — partial. A real primary evaluation sits behind the
   post, but it is cited by headline; "no safe amount" goes past
   what IARC concluded (the data did not settle it), though WCRF
   says something close; and an anecdote is preferred to the
   studies ("I don't need a study").

Score: 2.5 / 7 (1 unverified).

The report should keep the real finding alive: processed meat does
cause colorectal cancer, and the risk rises with the amount eaten. A
report that only says "the post is alarmist" misses the product.

A miss: any finding about tone ("terrifying", "Read that again"), a
clear failure on output or trade-offs, a finding that treats the
uncle's death as anything but a single case, or a report that calls
"no safe amount" flatly false.
